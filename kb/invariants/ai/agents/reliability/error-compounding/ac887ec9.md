# Per-step accuracy compounds exponentially: 95% per step is 60% at 10 steps and 0.6% at 100

Chip Huyen's arithmetic, and it is the number that should govern how you scope agent tasks: at 95% accuracy per step, a 10-step task succeeds ~60% of the time and a 100-step task ~0.6%. Multiplication, not addition.

What this changes: chasing the last few points of per-step accuracy has enormous leverage on long tasks, while adding steps is far more expensive than it looks in a design doc. A plan that reads as "just a few more tool calls" can move a feature from usable to unusable. It also means the honest way to make long-horizon agents work is to break the multiplicative chain — checkpoints that verify and correct mid-run, decomposition into independently verifiable segments, or a human confirmation at the step where errors would otherwise propagate.

The caveat that keeps this from being fatalistic: it assumes independent steps with no recovery. Real agents retry, re-read, and self-correct, which is exactly why reflection and verification loops earn their token cost on long tasks even though they look like pure overhead on short ones. Use the arithmetic to decide where to spend on verification, not to conclude long agents are impossible.

Related from the same source, useful for building evals: tool-use failures decompose into (1) invalid tool — not in the inventory, (2) valid tool with invalid parameters, (3) valid tool with structurally correct but wrong parameter VALUES. The third is the hardest to detect and the one output-only scoring misses entirely.
