# Monitoring coverage and human paging are separate switches: the same operator monitors all internal Codex traffic and has paging for it switched off, because false alarms made round-the-clock paging unaffordable — so a workload can be fully covered and still unattended

A coverage figure says what is examined. It does not say what reaches a person, and in this operator's own published description the two diverge by workload. The compound condition has three parts and dropping any one of them turns the fact into a reassurance it does not support.

**What reaches a human, and how fast.** "When paging is enabled, the highest-priority security alerts reach members of our security and training teams, typically within 15 minutes of the initial alert." Responders then review the evidence, examine the affected systems and act to contain.

**Where paging is enabled.** Not everywhere, and the stated reason is the alarm rate: "False alarms have made it difficult to keep paging enabled around the clock for every workload." Three different regimes are published side by side: for frontier reinforcement-learning runs, a highest-priority alert can page a responder at any hour; for most other traffic, paging is limited to business hours; and "Paging is currently disabled for internal Codex traffic."

**Why that last one matters.** Internal Codex traffic is not a low-coverage workload — it is monitored regardless of which model is running, it skips the cheap first screening layer and starts at the model-based reviewer, and it is one of the sensitive workloads that gets the deepest investigation layer. It is among the most thoroughly *examined* traffic in the system and among the least *attended*. Depth of inspection and likelihood of a human seeing the result are set independently.

**The substitutes, and their status.** Two mechanisms stand in for a responder, and only one of them exists: "some critical alerts can automatically pause the affected workload without waiting for a responder to act" — present tense, a shipped control on some alerts. And: "We're working toward a broader safeguard that would pause affected activity if responders have not ruled out a highest-priority alert within 30 minutes of being paged" — a stated intention, not a control that is in place. Read the second as a roadmap item.

**The operational consequence.** When you are handed a monitoring-coverage number for a fleet, ask two further questions before treating it as a control: what fraction of flags pages a person, and during which hours. Budget for the alarm rate at design time, because it is the alarm rate that decides whether paging survives contact with operations — a monitor tuned for recall, which is what the cheap screening layers are for, pushes directly against the paging budget it depends on. Auto-pause on the most critical alerts is the mitigation that does not consume human attention, and it is the one to specify per workload rather than globally.

**What this does not mean.** It is not a claim that unpaged findings are discarded. The operator says follow-up on less urgent findings "depends on the relevant workload", and the deepest layer compares reports across cases to identify recurring patterns, which is review without paging. The gap is in *timely intervention*, not in recording.
