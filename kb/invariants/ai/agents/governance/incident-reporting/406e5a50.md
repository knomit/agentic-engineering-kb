# Agent incident-reporting clocks are measured in hours, which makes a programmatic stop mechanism an engineering requirement, not a policy one

Four regulatory windows OWASP names, all of which assume continuous oversight rather than periodic audit. **A duration is meaningless without the event it starts from, so the anchors are in the table** — the original version of this fact listed only the durations, which is the defect corrected on 2026-08-15.

| Instrument | Window | Clock starts at |
|---|---|---|
| DORA | **4-hour** initial notification | classification of an ICT incident as *major* — and classification is itself due **within 24 hours of awareness** |
| NIS2 | 24-hour early warning | awareness |
| New York RAISE Act | 72-hour frontier reporting | *determination* |
| California SB 53 | 15-day window | — |

**THE DORA ROW IS THE ONE THAT MISLEADS, IN BOTH DIRECTIONS.** Read as "4 hours from awareness" it looks like the tightest clock in the table by a factor of six. It is not: the report states DORA "requires initial notification within 4 hours of classifying an ICT incident as major, with classification itself due within 24 hours of awareness", so the worst-case path from awareness to notification is up to **28 hours**. But the 4-hour sub-clock is real and it starts on an event *your own triage produces*, which means a classification decision made at hour 23 leaves four hours, not twenty-eight. Neither the relaxed reading nor the panicked one is right; the two-stage structure is the thing to design against.

**WHY THE COARSER NUMBER IS EASY TO PICK UP:** the report states these clocks **twice, at two different resolutions** — once as a one-line summary in the governance overview, and once with the anchors in the regulatory chapter. The summary sentence is the one that surfaces first on a search. If you cite this document, cite the detailed passage.

The report's regulatory landscape covers **42 instruments across 10 jurisdictions**; these four are the ones it highlights as setting the pace.

**Why this belongs in an engineering knowledge base rather than a compliance one:** a four-hour external notification clock cannot be satisfied by quarterly audit or by log review on request. Meeting it requires live monitoring of agent behaviour, baselines that flag drift, automated incident routing, and — the load-bearing one — "stop mechanisms that operate in seconds rather than days" (verbatim). If your agent has no programmatic kill path a responder can pull *without shipping a deploy*, the shortest of these clocks is unreachable regardless of what your policy documents say. Design the stop control, the behavioural baseline and the routing hook in at build time; they cannot be retrofitted inside a four-hour window.

**Scope caveat, because the numbers are not universal.** Applicability is sector- and jurisdiction-bound: DORA covers EU financial entities, NIS2 EU essential and important entities, and RAISE and SB 53 are frontier-model developer obligations in New York and California respectively. The report notes these regimes cover **different entity types** — DORA and NIS2 land on financial and critical-infrastructure *deployers*, RAISE and SB 53 on frontier model *developers* — so an organisation that both builds and deploys agents faces genuinely overlapping windows. Treat these as the tightest plausible clocks to design against, not as binding on every deployment. These figures are reported in a governance survey rather than read from the instruments themselves — verify the specific one that applies to you before relying on the number.
