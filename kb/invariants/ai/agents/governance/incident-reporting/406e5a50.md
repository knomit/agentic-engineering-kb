# Agent incident-reporting clocks are measured in hours, which makes a programmatic stop mechanism an engineering requirement, not a policy one

Four regulatory windows OWASP names, all of which assume continuous oversight rather than periodic audit:

| Instrument | Window |
|---|---|
| DORA | 4-hour notification |
| NIS2 | 24-hour early warning |
| New York RAISE Act | 72-hour frontier reporting |
| California SB 53 | 15-day window |

The report's regulatory landscape covers 42 instruments across 10 jurisdictions; these are the ones it highlights as setting the pace.

**Why this belongs in an engineering knowledge base rather than a compliance one:** a 4-hour external notification clock cannot be satisfied by quarterly audit or by log review on request. Meeting it requires live monitoring of agent behaviour, baselines that flag drift, automated incident routing, and — the load-bearing one — "stop mechanisms that operate in seconds rather than days." If your agent has no programmatic kill path a responder can pull *without shipping a deploy*, the shortest of these clocks is unreachable regardless of what your policy documents say. Design the stop control, the behavioural baseline and the routing hook in at build time; they cannot be retrofitted inside a four-hour window.

**Scope caveat, because the numbers are not universal.** Applicability is sector- and jurisdiction-bound: DORA covers EU financial entities, NIS2 EU essential and important entities, and RAISE and SB 53 are frontier-model developer obligations in New York and California respectively. Treat these as the tightest plausible clocks to design against, not as binding on every deployment. These figures are also reported in a governance survey rather than read from the instruments themselves — verify the specific one that applies to you before relying on the number.
