# A capability eval's headline number is a lower bound set by its prompt — prompt engineering alone moved chain-of-thought constraint compliance from 5.5% to 15% on one model, and ~2-3x across open-source models

Redwood Research re-ran CoTControl — which measures whether reasoning models can obey formatting constraints inside their chain-of-thought while answering multiple-choice questions — under better prompts, and reports that "using better prompts improves performance for open-source models by ~2-3x or more", "e.g. from 5.5% to 15% for GPT-OSS-120B". Setup: four models (Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-32B, GPT-OSS-20B, GPT-OSS-120B), questions from GPQA, HLE and MMLU-Pro at temperature 1.0, "Each was evaluated on 50 samples per mode", over the nine original modes plus ten held-out modes the author added. The conditions compared were the original one-sentence constraint, zero-shot variants (repeating the rule around the question, a self-monitor instruction, a stacked system directive plus example), and few-shot with compliant traces prepended, both on-policy and out-of-distribution.

The design detail that makes this a measurement rather than a tuning anecdote: prompts were iterated on a small named subset of modes — "across a subset of modes (specifically `lowercase_thinking`, `word_suppression`, and `repeat_sentences`)" — and the winners were then applied to the remaining modes and to the held-out modes, so generalisation is tested on modes the prompts were never tuned against.

Operational consequence: a low score on a capability or dangerous-capability eval licenses no claim about the model until the elicitation has been attacked as hard as the model. Before publishing or acting on a negative result, run at least a repetition condition, a self-monitor instruction, and few-shot examples carrying compliant traces; tune on a held-back subset of conditions and report the result on the rest; and state which elicitation produced the number, because the number names a (model, prompt) pair and not a model.

What this does not mean, and the author is explicit about it: the underlying safety conclusion is not overturned. The post states that "current models probably can't consistently evade CoT monitoring across training, evaluation, and deployment", on the grounds that these eval tasks are far easier than subverting a production CoT monitor. The correction is to the number's status as a bound, not to the threat assessment resting on it. The comparison with METR's fine-tuning experiment is described as not very direct, because that fine-tuning was further out of distribution.

Confounds the post tracks rather than assumes away: few-shot conditions shorten reasoning and reduce accuracy, and shorter chains may be easier to control; one Qwen3-8B result may be inflated by that. Out-of-distribution few-shot examples peak early and are not monotonic in the number of examples.
