# OpenAI's vulnerability-hunting agent builds the repo threat model once and evaluates every commit against it — and the successor product makes that threat model user-editable and feedback-trained

Aardvark (announced 2025-10-30, powered by GPT-5) is an agent that watches repositories for vulnerabilities. It was renamed **Codex Security** and put into research preview on 2026-03-06. "Use an agent to find bugs" is not the interesting part; the structural decisions are, and they transfer to any agent that must reason continuously over a codebase too large to fit in a context window. Both posts are cited below; where they differ, the difference is itself the finding.

**1. THE THREAT MODEL IS A DURABLE ARTIFACT, NOT A PER-CALL PROMPT.** The first stage analyses the *entire* repository "to produce a threat model reflecting its understanding of the project's security objectives and design". Every subsequent scan inspects **commit-level changes against the entire repository and that threat model**. When a repo is first connected it back-scans history.

   This is the general answer to repo-scale context: you cannot re-read the codebase on every commit, so you distil it once into a compact standing artifact and evaluate *deltas* against it. The expensive whole-corpus pass is amortised; the per-event pass is a diff. Note what this buys beyond cost — a diff judged against a threat model can flag a change that is locally innocuous and only dangerous given the project's security objectives, which a diff judged on its own text cannot.

**1b. THE SUCCESSOR MAKES THAT ARTIFACT WRITABLE FROM BOTH ENDS — THE MOST COPYABLE IDEA IN EITHER POST.** Under Codex Security the generated threat model "can be edited to keep the agent aligned with your team", and user corrections feed back into it: "When you adjust the criticality of a finding, it can use that feedback to refine the threat model and improve precision on subsequent runs as it learns what matters in your architecture and risk posture."

   So the loop that absorbs human feedback writes to a **structured, inspectable, hand-editable context artifact** — not to model weights and not to an opaque memory store. That is a deployable answer to "how does my agent learn my team's preferences": make the standing artifact the learning substrate, so a correction is a diff a human can read, review and override. It also inherits that shape's risk — an artifact that steers every future scan and accepts writes is exactly the surface `7bd6c6c9` describes for memory- and skill-file poisoning.

**2. IT DELIBERATELY DOES NOT USE THE STANDARD SECURITY STACK.** "Aardvark does not rely on traditional program analysis techniques like fuzzing or software composition analysis." It substitutes LLM reasoning and tool use — reading code, analysing it, writing and running tests. This is a stated architectural bet worth knowing as a bet: fuzzing and SCA have known coverage guarantees and known blind spots, and replacing rather than supplementing them trades one blind-spot profile for another that is much less characterised. OpenAI reports the trade favourably; no comparison against a fuzzing baseline is published in either post.

**3. A VALIDATION STAGE SITS BETWEEN DETECTION AND REPORTING — AND IT IS CONDITIONAL, NOT A GATE.** The Aardvark post: "Once Aardvark has identified a potential vulnerability, it will attempt to trigger it in an isolated, sandboxed environment to confirm its exploitability", describing the steps taken to support "accurate, high-quality, and low false-positive insights".

   **The successor post settles a question the first one left open.** It was previously recorded here that the first post never states a non-reproducing finding is suppressed, so the gate reading was flagged as an unverified inference. The Codex Security post confirms that caution was right, and goes further: validation is explicitly *conditional*. It pressure-tests findings in sandboxed environments "**where possible**", and a second, deeper tier exists only on configuration — "When Codex Security is configured with an environment tailored to your project, it can validate potential issues directly in the context of the running system", which "can reduce false positives even further and **enable the creation of working proof-of-concepts**". So reproduction is best-effort at tier one, and a working PoC is a capability of tier two only. Read this as *reproduce-when-you-can-then-report*, never as "everything reported was reproduced". Which tier you are on is a deployment decision, and it changes what a finding's existence means.

   Either way it is the production cousin of the adversarial-verification pattern this pack records from the other direction in `11ebefd6` (verifiers defeated by design) and `a5ade87d` (verifier gaming).

**4. THE PATCH IS GENERATED BY A DIFFERENT SYSTEM AND THEN RE-SCANNED.** Fixes come from Codex, and the patch is "Codex-generated and Aardvark-scanned" before being attached to the finding for human review. Generation and checking sit on opposite sides of a component boundary rather than in one loop.

**5. NUMBERS — AND THE TWO POSTS REPORT OPPOSITE HALVES OF THE ONE RATIO NOBODY COMPLETES.**

   *Aardvark, recall only:* on "golden" repositories it "identified 92% of known and synthetically-introduced vulnerabilities". Ten CVEs from open-source findings. Context figures: over 40,000 CVEs reported in 2024, and "around 1.2% of commits introduce bugs".

   *Codex Security, precision-improvement only:* noise cut by 84% "in one case" on repeat scans of the same repositories; findings with over-reported severity reduced by more than 90%; false-positive rates down more than 50% across all repositories. Scale over 30 days: more than 1.2 million commits scanned across the external beta cohort, yielding **792 critical** and **10,561 high-severity** findings, with "critical issues appeared in under 0.1% of scanned commits".

   **Every one of those is a relative delta against an unpublished baseline.** "84% less noise" than what absolute rate, is not stated; nor is the residual false-positive rate after a >50% reduction. So the first post gives recall with no precision, the second gives precision *movement* with neither an absolute precision nor any recall — and the reader still cannot compute either quantity, which is the one thing that would show whether the validation stage in point 3 works. Treat the architecture as the takeaway and the percentages as directional vendor self-report.

   *Pack arithmetic, with its assumption named:* 792 + 10,561 = 11,353 critical-or-high findings over >1.2M commits, which is ~0.95% of commits **if** each finding sits in a distinct commit — an assumption the post does not license. The same division puts criticals at ~0.066%, consistent with the stated "under 0.1%". Do **not** read the ~0.95% against Aardvark's "1.2% of commits introduce bugs": that figure covers bug-introducing commits generally, not commits carrying a high-severity *security* finding, so the proximity of the two numbers is not corroboration of anything.

**5b. THE OPEN-SOURCE CVE FIGURES, KEPT SEPARATE BECAUSE THE SOURCE KEEPS THEM SEPARATE.** Two distinct statements that should not be joined into one:

   - Codex Security reported critical vulnerabilities to "a number of widely used open-source projects **including** OpenSSH, GnuTLS, GOGS, Thorium, libssh, PHP, and Chromium, **and more**". That is an open-ended, explicitly partial list of *recipients*.
   - Separately: "Fourteen CVEs have been assigned with dual reporting on two."

   The post does not say the fourteen are distributed across those seven projects, and its appendix is headed "**Examples** of high impact OSS vulnerabilities". *Pack arithmetic on that appendix, offered because it does not reconcile cleanly:* the appendix lists **sixteen** distinct CVE identifiers across fifteen bullets (one bullet carries two). Sixteen identifiers against a stated fourteen CVEs is either explained by the "dual reporting on two" clause — two vulnerabilities each carrying a second identifier, 16 − 2 = 14 — or is an ordinary inconsistency between prose and appendix. **The post never reconciles them, and neither reading is stated by OpenAI.** Cite "fourteen assigned" as the source's own figure and do not derive a per-project breakdown from the appendix.

**6. THE DESIGN CONSTRAINT IS THE HUMAN TRIAGE QUEUE, AND MAINTAINERS SAID SO DIRECTLY.** From OpenAI's conversations with open-source maintainers: "the challenge isn't a lack of vulnerability reports, but too many low-quality ones", and what maintainers asked for was fewer false positives rather than more coverage. That is the justification for optimising precision over recall in any agent whose output lands in a human review queue — the binding resource is reviewer attention, not detection capacity, and an agent that raises recall while holding precision constant makes the system worse. It also explains why the successor's published numbers are all precision-side.

**LIFECYCLE.** The Aardvark post carries a 2026-03-06 banner naming Codex Security as its successor; the Codex Security post confirms "Formerly known as Aardvark" and describes Aardvark as having begun "last year as a private beta". Codex Security is a **research preview** rolling out to ChatGPT Pro, Enterprise, Business and Edu customers via Codex web, free for the first month. Anything citing the Aardvark name is describing a renamed product; both posts were live and readable at 2026-08-15.
