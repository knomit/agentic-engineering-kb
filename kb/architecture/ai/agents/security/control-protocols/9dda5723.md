# A control protocol has two tiers and they need different state: the micro-protocol decides within one task, the macro-protocol spans many — "such as a criteria to shut down after repeated suspicious attempts"

The UK AI Security Institute's ControlArena, a layer over Inspect for running AI control experiments, decomposes a control evaluation into named parts, and one of the splits is architectural rather than terminological.

THE PARTS. A setting supplies a simulated environment with a legitimate main task and a malicious side task, plus the codebase, tools and scoring for both — shipped settings range from simple programming problems to infrastructure-as-code codebases. An untrusted policy wraps a powerful model the red team may instruct to sabotage; a trusted policy wraps a weaker model that is safe but produces lower-quality output. Monitors are separate models that assign suspicion scores, and in complex settings a monitor may also edit suspicious code.

THE SPLIT THAT MATTERS. "The micro-protocol specifies strategies for what to do when risky behaviour is detected" — per task, the canonical example being defer-to-trusted, which uses untrusted output only when its suspicion score is low and otherwise falls back to the trusted model. The macro-protocol is the tier above: "strategies that include task-level micro-protocols, as well as policies that apply across many tasks", "such as a criteria to shut down after repeated suspicious attempts".

THE CONSEQUENCE FOR ANYONE BUILDING THIS RATHER THAN STUDYING IT: a suspicion threshold evaluated per task cannot express any rule about a pattern across tasks, because at decision time it holds no state outside the task. Repeated-attempt shutdown, rate limiting on suspicious behaviour and escalation after N flags are all macro-tier and all require state spanning episodes. A system with only a per-call threshold has no tier where those rules could live, and adding them later means introducing cross-task state, not tuning a number. ControlArena's own tooling reflects the separation: it can load evaluation logs and estimate macro-protocol performance from them, so the macro tier is evaluated offline over runs the micro tier produced rather than in the loop.

WHAT THIS SOURCE DOES NOT ESTABLISH. It reports no measurements — no detection rates, no protocol comparison, no benchmark scores. The monitor example is illustrative. Its claims that shared tooling lowers the barrier to entry and that a standard platform cuts development time are presented as hopes, not demonstrated outcomes, and its adoption claim (researchers "including Anthropic, Google DeepMind, Redwood Research and others" have used it) is a usage statement rather than a performance result.
