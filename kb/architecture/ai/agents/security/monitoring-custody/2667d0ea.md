# Who stores monitoring data, who analyses it and who adjudicates a flag are three separable roles — splitting them dissolves the apparent choice between vendor monitoring and zero data retention

Vendor-side misuse monitoring is usually posed as a binary: either the provider retains your traffic and can correlate it, or it retains nothing and cannot. An enterprise design published by one model provider takes that apart by separating three roles that the binary collapses into one actor — **who holds the data, who runs the detection, and who decides what a flag means**.

THE SPLIT AS BUILT:
- **Custody stays with the customer.** "Activity data used for monitoring can be stored in the customer's own cloud account (such as Amazon S3, Azure Blob Storage, or Google Cloud Storage)", "under their own encryption keys, access policies, and audit logging."
- **Detection stays with the vendor.** "automated systems analyze a rolling window of traffic for signals of serious misuse, including attempts to develop offensive cyber or biological capabilities and signs of stolen or leaked credentials."
- **Adjudication moves to the customer.** "Those flags go directly to the customer and their people take it from there – no human review by Anthropic employees is required."

THE REASON ADJUDICATION IS THE ROLE THAT HAD TO MOVE is the useful part, because it is not about trust in the abstract. Human review still earns its place — "Even as automated review is becoming more effective, a person looking at a flag still adds value by confirming real misuse and clearing false positives" — but WHO may perform it is externally constrained: "the person doing that review needs to be one of their own. Many operate under rules that tightly govern who may see certain information—privileged legal material, non-public information, drug-safety reports. Their teams are already trained and cleared for that work." An automated detector has no clearance problem; a human reviewer does. So the role that cannot be outsourced is the one with a person in it.

**THE DESIGN QUESTIONS THIS YIELDS**, and they are quoted from an industry body's own summary of what it asked for: "who holds the data, who holds the keys, what automated review can and cannot see, and under what conditions a human is ever permitted to look." Those four are worth asking of any monitoring arrangement, in-house or vendor, because most designs answer them with one actor by default rather than by decision.

TWO CONFIGURATION PROPERTIES WORTH COPYING: each capability is independently elective — "Customer-owned storage, Customer-Managed Encryption Keys, and fully automated review are each opt-in" — and the monitoring layer is explicitly orthogonal to behaviour: "None of them change model behavior, API pricing, or rate limits." A monitoring option that silently changes throughput or refusal behaviour is a different product, and saying so is part of the design.

**MODALITY, AND IT IS THE MAIN LIMIT ON THIS FACT.** This is an announcement of a stated architecture, not an audited or deployed system: it "will be rolling out to customers in phases, starting later this fall", eligible customers get zero data retention on the current models "until EFS is ready", and much of the supporting text is in future tense. There is no effectiveness measurement, no detection rate and no independent assessment. What IS established is that more than a hundred enterprise customers and three cloud providers specified these requirements and that a provider committed to this shape — evidence about the requirements, not about the system.
