# Who stores monitoring data, who analyses it and who adjudicates a flag are three separable roles — splitting them dissolves the apparent choice between vendor monitoring and zero data retention

Vendor-side misuse monitoring is usually posed as a binary: either the provider retains your traffic and can correlate it, or it retains nothing and cannot. An enterprise design published by one model provider takes that apart by separating three roles that the binary collapses into one actor — **who holds the data, who runs the detection, and who decides what a flag means**.

THE SPLIT AS BUILT:
- **Custody stays with the customer.** "Activity data used for monitoring can be stored in the customer's own cloud account (such as Amazon S3, Azure Blob Storage, or Google Cloud Storage)", "under their own encryption keys, access policies, and audit logging."
- **Detection stays with the vendor.** "automated systems analyze a rolling window of traffic for signals of serious misuse, including attempts to develop offensive cyber or biological capabilities and signs of stolen or leaked credentials."
- **Adjudication moves to the customer.** "Those flags go directly to the customer and their people take it from there – no human review by Anthropic employees is required."

THE REASON ADJUDICATION IS THE ROLE THAT HAD TO MOVE is the useful part, because it is not about trust in the abstract. Human review still earns its place — "Even as automated review is becoming more effective, a person looking at a flag still adds value by confirming real misuse and clearing false positives" — but WHO may perform it is externally constrained: "the person doing that review needs to be one of their own. Many operate under rules that tightly govern who may see certain information—privileged legal material, non-public information, drug-safety reports. Their teams are already trained and cleared for that work." An automated detector has no clearance problem; a human reviewer does. So the role that cannot be outsourced is the one with a person in it.

**THE DESIGN QUESTIONS THIS YIELDS**, in the words of a customer testimonial carried on the page: "Eight of our members worked with Anthropic to define what it would take to run the most capable frontier models" "inside a systemically important bank: who holds the data, who holds the keys," "what automated review can and cannot see, and under what conditions a human is ever permitted to look." Those four questions are worth asking of any monitoring arrangement, in-house or vendor, because most designs answer them with one actor by default rather than by decision.

ATTRIBUTION, because that quote invites an over-claim. The page prints it against a person's name and title — "Scott DePasquale", "President and CEO" — with no organization printed beside it. "Our members" implies a membership body, and the article separately names the Analysis and Resilience Center for Systemic Risk (ARC) among the groups it worked with, but the page never joins the quote to that body. So this is one named speaker's account of what his members asked for, not a published position of any named organization, and it should not be cited as an industry body's requirements summary.

TWO CONFIGURATION PROPERTIES WORTH COPYING: each capability is independently elective — "Customer-owned storage, Customer-Managed Encryption Keys, and fully automated review are each opt-in" — and the monitoring layer is explicitly orthogonal to behaviour: "None of them change model behavior, API pricing, or rate limits." A monitoring option that silently changes throughput or refusal behaviour is a different product, and saying so is part of the design.

**MODALITY, AND IT IS THE MAIN LIMIT ON THIS FACT.** This is an announcement of a stated architecture, not an audited or deployed system: it "will be rolling out to customers in phases, starting later this fall", and "eligible customers will receive ZDR on Fable 5 and Fable 5.1 until EFS is ready" — two named models, not the current roster in general — with much of the supporting text in future tense. No general-availability date appears anywhere on the page: the getting-started section says only that EFS "will roll out to customers in phases," "with the goal of making it broadly available later this fall", and access is something the reader requests through a form rather than something the page grants. There is no effectiveness measurement, no detection rate and no independent assessment. What IS established is narrower than "this population specified these requirements". The page says EFS was developed "in close collaboration with more than 100 customers in industries like financial services", "healthcare, manufacturing, telecom, law, retail, and the public sector," "and with our cloud partners at Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud, and Microsoft Azure." The three cloud partners are named rather than counted, and collaboration is what is claimed, not specification. So the evidence is that a provider committed publicly to this shape after working with that population — evidence about the commitment, not about a running system.
