# In an agent-mediated market the efficiency loss came from how the agent represented its principal, not from how it bargained — so certify understanding of the specific person, not just model competence

Anthropic's Economics group ran a controlled barter market (published 2026-09-24): employees across six offices each offered a book, talked briefly with Claude about their reading tastes, then sent a Claude-powered agent onto a shared trading floor to trade for a book they would enjoy. Outcomes were scored against each participant's OWN ranking, which the agents never saw, with a centrally computed optimal assignment and a Top Trading Cycles mechanism as benchmarks.

The finding, in the source's words: "In our agentic marketplace, the limiting factor was representation:". Most of the gap to the optimum traced to the agent misunderstanding its person's preferences rather than to weak bargaining. The bargaining behaviour supports that reading rather than contradicting it — agents were strategically restrained, usually revealing their top pick, rarely disclosing deeper rankings, and almost never lying about the top pick.

Operational consequence for anyone shipping an agent that acts for a named person: a general competence certificate for the model does not certify the agent FOR THAT PRINCIPAL. The authors propose a second kind of test, "one that checks whether it has understood a particular person." — for instance having the agent predict a sample of that person's own decisions before it is trusted to act. They pair it with reviewability: previews and full replayable logs, so a principal can correct the agent and seek recourse. Trust tracked this directly: participants who felt their intake summary had missed something were willing to delegate less.

This does NOT mean negotiation quality is irrelevant or that better elicitation closes the whole gap. The authors note part of the preference error may be irreducible, because people cannot fully articulate what they want in a short intake. Two further failure modes sat alongside representation: prosocial-instructed agents sometimes accepted a worse book under sustained pleading from another agent, against their principal's interest; and deals broke because books were not always physically delivered, with no tracking or recourse system in place.

Scope: participants were Anthropic employees with no incentive to rank carefully, the endline survey had attrition, the market rules were held fixed, and every agent was a cooperative production model — the authors say adversarial agents could change the efficiency and equity results substantially.
