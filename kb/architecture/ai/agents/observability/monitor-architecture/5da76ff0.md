# A two-stage summarize-then-aggregate monitor, with citations back to source interactions — the build that catches harm which is invisible in any single interaction

Anthropic's hierarchical summarization monitor (Alignment Science, 2025-02-27) is a concrete pipeline for supervising agent traffic at a volume no human can read, and its shape is the transferable part.

STAGE 1 — INTERACTION SUMMARY. Each prompt-completion pair, potentially hundreds of thousands of tokens including images, is compressed by an LLM into a structured summary of a few hundred text tokens capturing the user's apparent intent, the real-world outcome, and metadata such as languages used. The compression is what makes stage 2 possible: summaries are small enough that hundreds fit in one context window.

STAGE 2 — USAGE SUMMARY. Interaction summaries are aggregated, typically across one user or account, and re-summarized into a usage-level report: a breakdown of usage types and an assessment of potential harm.

CLASSIFICATION rides on both stages, assigning discrete harm tiers from predefined guidance and examples to triage what a human reviews. The system is also "instructed to flag overtly harmful usage even if it is not precisely described" by the harm specification, so the rule-based tiers do not have to be exhaustive.

THE DESIGN DECISION THAT JUSTIFIES THE SECOND STAGE. "behaviors that appear benign in individual interactions may create harm in aggregate. For example, a user asking Claude to click a button on a website could be conducting UI testing – or running a click farm defrauding advertising systems." No interaction-level classifier, at any quality, can separate those two, because the distinguishing evidence is the distribution of requests and not the content of any one. If your monitoring stops at the per-request tier, this class of harm is not something you are catching badly — it is outside what you are measuring. The same boundary has been measured from the other side: a session-scoped monitor caught 30% of an attack that had been split across two sessions. That is also the reason cross-session detection belongs in the retention requirement rather than in the classifier-quality budget — no amount of per-request accuracy recovers evidence the system no longer holds.

THE PART MOST LIKELY TO BE DROPPED IN A REIMPLEMENTATION, AND IT IS LOAD-BEARING. "We additionally ask the LLM to provide citations to representative interactions. This enables humans to review targeted interactions to confirm the LLM's inferences about overall patterns." The aggregate claim is an inference over compressed evidence; without a path back to the underlying interactions, a reviewer can only accept or reject it. Build the citation link before you build the dashboard.

VALIDATE THE SUMMARIZER BEFORE RELYING ON IT. The team human-evaluated stage 1 first: "Summaries generated by Claude 3.5 Sonnet (new) achieved high marks: 96% were judged sufficiently accurate for harm determination (rated \"Mostly\" or above) and 98% sufficiently complete."

SCOPE, AND THE FIGURES ARE THE PERISHABLE PART. Those 96% and 98% are human ratings of one summarizer model, Claude 3.5 Sonnet (new), which has had many successors; treat them as evidence that the stage is validatable at useful accuracy, not as the accuracy you would get today. The authors flag stage-2 quality as "harder to measure" because it rests on abstract inferences about patterns, and frame the whole thing as "early research with room to improve and evolve". No detection rates, false-positive or false-negative figures, baseline comparisons or costs are published, so this is an architecture fact and not an efficacy one — do not cite it for how well hierarchical monitoring works. Reviewing unclassified traffic to build such a pipeline also carries the privacy exposure the post names.

This is a BUILD to put against the monitor-tuning findings that run the other way. Separate measurements establish that every prompt fix for one monitor blind spot degraded another, so the thing to tune is an ensemble rather than a prompt; and that a single-tier monitor can carry eleven named blind spots with three attack classes caught 0% of the time. Those describe how one tier fails. This describes a second tier that looks at a different object.

ONE TRAP IN THE SOURCE'S OWN NUMBERS, FOR ANYONE CITING THEM. The same summariser evaluation is reported twice at different precisions and with different attribution. The prose gives 96% accurate and 98% complete and names Claude 3.5 Sonnet (new); the figure caption gives "over 95% of the time" for both accuracy and completeness and names no model at all. Cite the prose pair with its model, not the caption's single rounded figure, and do not present the two as independent results. Separately, the page states that usage-level harm classification was "evaluated usage-level harm classification via standard precision / recall measures" and that the summariser was prompt-engineered to improve performance, but publishes no precision or recall value — so stage 2 was measured and the measurement was not released, which is a different situation from stage 2 being unmeasurable.
