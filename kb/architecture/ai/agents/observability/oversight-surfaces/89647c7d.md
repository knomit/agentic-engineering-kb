# An oversight review names four surfaces — activations, chain-of-thought, external actions and inter-agent communication — and says the properties needed to detect their degradation are not routinely tracked

UK AISI's review of whether AI oversight will get harder "identifies four oversight surfaces": internal activations, chain-of-thought, external actions, and inter-agent communication. It treats the current techniques — action monitoring, chain-of-thought reading, behavioural evaluations, activation probing, and training-based approaches that cut across surfaces — as layered defences over those surfaces rather than as alternatives. It "identifies more than twenty distinct pathways by which current oversight could degrade", some of which could compound, and it states that "many oversight-relevant properties are not routinely tracked". Pathways it names include models recognising that they are being tested and changing behaviour, latent reasoning architectures moving reasoning out of readable text, the limits of action-only monitoring where control protocols such as blocking, rolling back or resampling are needed, and uncertainty about whether alignment honeypots and oversight-oriented training transfer to deployment. Its recommendation is that "developers can take steps to preserve current oversight channels and invest in emerging techniques as fallbacks".

Operational consequence: use the four surfaces as a coverage checklist for a monitoring design and state which of them your system actually reads. Inter-agent communication is a surface in its own right, so a multi-agent system whose monitors read only each agent's own actions and chain-of-thought has a channel nobody watches. And because the degradation this review describes is gradual rather than event-like, the properties that would reveal it have to be in your per-release record — chain-of-thought readability and length, monitor detection rate on a frozen suite, whether activations are available to you at all — otherwise the loss is only discoverable after it has happened.

Scope, and what this is not: the review is qualitative. It "draws on 25 expert interviews across frontier AI developers, government, NGOs, and academia" plus a literature review and the authors' own analysis, and it reports no measurements of model behaviour or of detection performance — the interview and pathway counts are its only numbers. So it is a map of what could degrade and a recommendation about what to preserve, not evidence that any surface has degraded or a measurement of how exposed any particular system is.
