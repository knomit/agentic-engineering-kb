# Shuffle sharding: assigning each tenant a random combination of workers beats plain sharding by orders of magnitude, at no extra cost

Plain sharding divides a worker fleet into fixed groups, so blast radius falls from 100% to 1/number-of-shards. Shuffle sharding instead assigns each tenant a randomly chosen COMBINATION of workers, and blast radius falls to 1/number-of-combinations, which grows combinatorially rather than linearly.

Amazon's worked example: 8 workers, each tenant assigned 2. Plain sharding into 4 shards of 2 gives a 25% blast radius. Shuffle sharding over the same 8 workers gives C(8,2) = 28 distinct shards, so a poison-pill request or a DDoS from one tenant affects 1/28 — about 7x better for identical hardware. Any other tenant overlaps the affected tenant on at most one of its two workers, so it keeps serving from the other.

At real scale the effect is extreme. Route 53 arranges capacity into 2048 virtual name servers and assigns each customer domain 4 of them, yielding roughly 730 billion possible shuffle shards — enough that every domain gets a unique shard, and Amazon can further guarantee no two customer domains share more than 2 virtual name servers. A DDoS against one domain spikes its 4 virtual name servers and no other customer notices.

Two conditions the technique depends on, which are the part to actually check:
1. Callers must be fault tolerant — retrying on failure — because the isolation guarantee is that an unaffected tenant retains SOME healthy workers, not that all of its workers stay healthy.
2. The workers must be interchangeable for the request in question, so any member of a tenant's shard can serve it.

Amazon's framing is that this is a way of arranging resources you already have, so it usually costs nothing, and that it gets more effective as workers and tenants grow — the opposite of most scaling techniques. Route 53's implementations are open-sourced in the Infima library, and Amazon also uses recursive shuffle sharding to isolate a customer's own customers at a second layer.

For agent platforms the unit to shard is the pool a tenant's runs are placed on — sandbox workers, browser or VM pools, queue consumers — and the failure it contains is precisely the agentic one: a single tenant's workload that reliably crashes or wedges whatever executes it.
