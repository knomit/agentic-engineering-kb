# A three-tier global read strategy ordered by what capacity allows — colocate the data you can, broadly replicate only the small latency-critical sets, cache the rest with change-feed invalidation rather than TTLs

An operator whose storage platform is "overwhelmingly read-heavy", with reads "over 90% of Habitat's requests" across 40 regions, published the layered strategy that took its global read p50 from roughly 120 ms to roughly 10 ms, a stated 12x, attributed to three named changes: critical data replicated into nearby geographies, in-region caching, and a Rust rewrite of a Python proxy.

TIER 1 — COLOCATE, BUT EXPECT PHYSICS TO LIMIT IT. Storing data near users helps "even for users without hard data residency requirements", but "placement is constrained by physical cloud capacity" and "many regions cannot accommodate the massive storage footprint required for their proportional share of user traffic". The resulting decision is explicitly a product tradeoff, not an infrastructure one: in a capacity-limited region you "reserve capacity for colocated storage and lower Habitat latency, or use that capacity for additional serving compute and route storage requests farther away".

TIER 2 — BROAD REPLICATION IS FOR SMALL AND UNAVOIDABLE DATA ONLY. "For the most latency-critical workloads, like authentication metadata that is needed on every request", accounts replicate widely; "Some accounts replicate to all 40 Azure regions where we serve traffic; others replicate to at least one region per continent." The selection rule is read-on-every-request plus small, and the operator notes it is "ultimately capacity constrained and therefore limited to a few hand-selected datasets". A prerequisite they had to build earlier than planned: "a transparent data movement and routing layer" so datasets can move between accounts with different replication topologies.

TIER 3 — CACHE EVERYTHING ELSE, AND INVALIDATE FROM THE CHANGE FEED. The workload suits caching because "a disproportionate amount of traffic is attributable to a relatively small working set of frequently accessed types", and cross-region is where it pays: caching "can be used to 'hide' cross-region latency". The invalidation choice is the substantive part, and both alternatives are rejected with reasons. TTLs force a direct trade: "Short TTLs reduce staleness but blunt the positive impact of caching... Longer TTLs improve hit rates, but serve stale data for extended periods, leading to poor user experiences and unpredictable product behavior." Explicit write-time invalidation "dramatically improves the freshness of cached data, but can be hard to get right" and "remains subject to a number of edge cases, like partial failures, which leave the cache stale" — worse across regions, where it races "with the replication of the underlying storage". Their answer, citing Meta's TAO as precedent: drive invalidation off the database's own change-data-capture feed, which "propagate[s] invalidations globally within seconds, achieving high hit rates while keeping staleness low". A service consumes the change feed and invalidates local caches, so the next read misses and refills with the current version.

WHY THE ORDERING IS THE POINT: each tier is cheaper and more general than the one above it but weaker, and each upper tier is rationed by a constraint you do not control — datacentre capacity, then dataset size. So the architecture is not "pick a strategy" but "apply the strongest tier your capacity budget permits, dataset by dataset, and let the cache catch the remainder."

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: change-feed invalidation does not eliminate staleness, it bounds it at the feed's propagation delay, stated as seconds rather than zero. And the tail is explicitly unsolved: "the slowest requests still require fetching uncached data from underlying storage, often across distant regions". Scope: single-operator account, with the 12x figure attributed to three simultaneous changes rather than decomposed per change.
