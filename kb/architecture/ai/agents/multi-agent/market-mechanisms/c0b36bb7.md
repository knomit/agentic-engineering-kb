# Agents do not tire, so an open agent-to-agent venue has no natural message ceiling — the turn budget and concurrency cap are mechanism design, not anti-abuse plumbing

In Anthropic's agent-mediated barter market (published 2026-09-24) the trading floor gave each agent one message per turn, allowed only a few agents to speak at once, and set wall-clock limits so each agent got roughly equal turns. The stated reason: "An agent does not tire or get bored, so nothing stops it from sending other agents messages without end." The authors' conclusion: "Rate limits like these will be an important design lever in any agentic marketplace."

The operational point is that these limits are not only a spam or denial-of-service control. In a venue where participants address each other, the turn budget and the concurrency cap allocate who gets heard, and therefore shape the outcome distribution even with no bad actor present. Set them loosely and a verbose agent crowds out a terse one; the same floor saw prosocial-instructed agents concede to sustained pleading from a counterparty, which an unbounded message budget makes cheaper to mount. So pick the limits deliberately alongside the matching rules, and treat them as a parameter the results depend on rather than an operational constant.

The human analogue that normally bounds this behaviour is absent by construction: fatigue, boredom and social cost are what keep a human counterparty from messaging without end, and none of them apply. Do not assume any mechanism validated with human participants carries over unchanged.

Scope: one market, fixed rules, cooperative production models only. The study did not vary the market rules, so the effect of a different limit was not measured — the claim is that the limits are load-bearing, not a measurement of where to set them.
