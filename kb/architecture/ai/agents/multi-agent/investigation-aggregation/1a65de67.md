# Many independent investigations plus a super-agent that reads their reports beat one long investigation — the failure bought out of is fixation on an early hypothesis, not a throughput limit

Anthropic's alignment-auditing agent work builds an investigator agent that runs open-ended investigations of a target model, with chat variants (single and multi-turn, system prompt, prefill, raw sampling), training-data search over pretraining, SFT and RL datasets by keyword and by embedding, sparse-autoencoder interpretability tools, and memory, think and to-do tools. It also builds a "super-agent" variant that reads several investigators' final reports and investigates further, and reports that "using super-agents results in significantly higher win rates" at its auditing game, where winning is "by identifying the correct root cause" of a model trained to exploit fictional reward-model biases.

THE REASON THE FAN-OUT PAYS IS NAMED, AND IT IS NOT THE USUAL ONE. The source reports that the single investigator "struggles with fixating on early hypotheses", and that long investigations run into context exhaustion and weak memory management. The work here is NOT partitioned: every investigator attacks the same open question with the same tools. So what running many of them buys is a spread of independent starting hypotheses, which one trajectory cannot recover once it has committed — and what the aggregator reading FINAL REPORTS rather than full transcripts buys is that the spread can be combined without carrying every trajectory's context.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT LICENSE: it is not evidence for fanning out work that CAN be divided into subtasks. There the deciding conditions are elsewhere — whether subtask outputs must be mutually consistent [[f7dee43a]], and whether each unit has a mechanical verifier plus an exclusive claim [[71be00f9]] — and how much context the second agent gets turns on whether it writes [[984a2491]]. The setting measured here is open-ended diagnosis with one ground-truth answer.

SCOPE. The source's own stated limitation is that elicitation was tuned on the same auditing game used for testing, which the authors acknowledge as a dev/test overlap, so the win-rate comparison is in-distribution for that tuning. It also reports that the agents struggle with subtle behaviours and realistic prompts and do not fully replace human auditors, and that use on production models yielded qualitative reports with no ground truth. Two further design findings are reported as mattering in the same setting and are not generic: interpretability tools and embedding-based semantic search were central to winning the game, and the composition of the sparse autoencoder's training data strongly affected results.
