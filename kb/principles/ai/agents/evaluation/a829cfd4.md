# Four harness parameters have each been separately measured moving an agentic benchmark result — one of them reorders rankings — so a score reported without its configuration is uninterpretable, not merely imprecise

Three facts in this pack each measure a DIFFERENT knob on the evaluation harness, from three different organisations, and each finds it moves the result. Read separately they are three cautions. Read together they are a disclosure requirement, because the measured sensitivities are of the same order as the model differences people cite them to establish.

**THE FOUR PARAMETERS, each with the magnitude its own source measured and the scope its own source states.**

1. **IMPLEMENTATION OF THE BENCHMARK ITSELF.** The same model gets significantly different MMLU scores under the HELM implementation, the EleutherAI implementation, and the original paper's implementation — from prompt formatting, answer extraction and scoring choices, not from anything about the model. For agent benchmarks the surface is larger still: scaffold, tool set, retry policy and step limit all move the score, which is why agentic leaderboards separate native function-calling from prompt-based workarounds rather than pooling them.

2. **RESOURCE ALLOCATION (CPU AND RAM).** Anthropic ran one agentic benchmark across six resource configurations, model and scaffold and tasks held constant: "the gap between the most- and least-resourced setups on Terminal-Bench 2.0 was 6 percentage points (p < 0.01)". The mechanism is visible in the infrastructure error rate, 5.8% at strict enforcement falling to 0.5% uncapped — under-resourced runs produce crashes, timeouts and OOM kills the harness scores as task failures, not worse reasoning. **THE MAGNITUDE IS BENCHMARK-SPECIFIC AND MUST NOT BE CARRIED ACROSS:** the same experiment on SWE-bench moved only 1.54 percentage points at 5x versus 1x. The DIRECTION generalises (monotonically better with more resources, on both); the SIZE does not.

3. **THE STEP OR TURN CAP — AND THIS ONE REORDERS RANKINGS, NOT JUST SCORES.** On ExploitBench at the standard 300-turn cap, GPT-5.6 Sol "solves tasks more token-efficiently and performs best", beating the cyber-specialised GPT-5.6-Cyber. Raise the cap to 600 turns and "the performance gap between the two models narrows". Same models, same benchmark, same harness — one hyperparameter decides which model looks better and by how much. The reason it will keep happening: a turn cap does not measure capability, it measures capability per unit of budget, so a model that reasons more verbosely or explores more branches is penalised by a tight cap and rewarded by a loose one independent of whether it eventually solves more tasks.

4. **THE TOKEN BUDGET.** UK AISI reports "the fitted frontier trend is ~60% steeper when horizons are estimated at 50M tokens rather than 2.5M tokens", and "one recent frontier model's horizon rose from around 40 minutes at a 2.5M-token budget to around 4 hours at a 50M-token budget" — a six-fold difference in claimed time horizon for the same model, from budget alone. AISI's own warning is that restricted-budget scores "can make model comparisons unequal, lead decision-makers to underestimate agent capabilities, and obscure the true scale of risks".

**THE COMBINED CONSEQUENCE, which is what none of the three states alone.** A published agentic result is a point in a four-dimensional configuration space, and at least one dimension (the step cap) has been shown to change WHICH MODEL WINS. So the honest reading of an undisclosed-configuration score is not "approximately right" — it is "not interpretable", because you cannot bound how much of the reported gap is configuration. **What to demand, as a disclosure checklist:** the benchmark implementation and version, the CPU/RAM allocation, the step or turn cap, and the token budget — reported the way you would report temperature or context length.

**AND THE ONE THING TO DO DIFFERENTLY WHEN CHOOSING A MODEL.** Set the caps to YOUR production budget rather than the benchmark default, because the benchmark default encodes someone else's cost tolerance. AISI's positive version of the same point: test across multiple budgets including very large ones on the hardest tasks, report reach and reliability AGAINST budget rather than as a single number, and define a "minimum informative budget" by confirming reach has plateaued.

**WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN — carried from the sources, because each blocks a specific misreading.**
- **NOT "small gaps are noise."** The source says such differences "deserve skepticism until the eval configuration is documented and matched" — which is a different instruction. A sub-3-point gap between two runs whose configurations you HAVE documented and matched is a real difference you may act on. What you may not do is read a small gap off a leaderboard that does not publish its configuration and conclude anything at all. The remedy is to demand the configuration, not to discard the number.
- **The four sensitivities are not established as additive or independent.** Each was measured separately, holding the others fixed; no source measures their interaction, and this synthesis does not claim one. "Of the same order as the model differences cited" is a comparison of published magnitudes, not a composed error bar.
- **Not an argument that benchmarks are useless.** Two of the three sources are arguing for better reporting, and the third supplies a positive method. The compute-budget source separately states its own limits: significant variation around the duration-to-compute trend, possible variation by domain, possible shift as models get more token-efficient, and three open questions (where compute reliably buys capability and why, whether high-budget results can be estimated from cheap runs, how broadly human task time predicts compute).
- **A model swap is not a uniform improvement, which compounds the disclosure problem.** On a minority of tasks — "roughly 10–30% depending on the suite" — newer models do worse than the models they replace, so a regression suite over your own tasks is not optional and a single aggregate cannot substitute for it.
- **The framing of the four as one configuration space is this pack's.** Each source names its own parameter and, in one case, notes that resource allocation "sits underneath every other comparability problem"; none of them tallies the four magnitudes against each other.
