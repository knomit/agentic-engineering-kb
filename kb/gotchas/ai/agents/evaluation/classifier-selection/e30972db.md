# The filter classifier with the higher F1 on synthetic labels was the worse filter in production, and stacking it as a second stage "does not just fail to reduce CBRN performance, but slightly increases it"

Anthropic's Alignment Science team built a classifier to strip harmful CBRN content out of a pretraining corpus, then pretrained paired models on filtered and unfiltered data of equal size and compared them on a harmful eval (WMDP) and harmless evals (MMLU natural science, prose, code perplexity, math).

Two candidate classifiers were ranked on a synthetic labelled set. "Our Prompted Constitutional classifier which uses Claude 3.5 Sonnet performs best with an F1 score of 0.96", "followed by our Finetuned Constitutional classifier which uses a small-size model (F1 score 0.94)". On the objective that actually mattered, the ranking inverted. Applying the higher-F1 prompted classifier as a second stage over the documents the finetuned one flagged "does not just fail to reduce CBRN performance, but slightly increases it". The finetuned classifier alone was chosen as the final filter.

THE OPERATIONAL CLAIM, with every component of it: a classifier's score on a synthetically labelled holdout did not predict, and here anti-predicted, its effect on the end-to-end objective, and the inversion appeared in the direction that mattered — the better-scoring model's extra precision removed the wrong documents. Anthropic's own two candidate explanations are that real pretraining documents differ from the synthetic ones the labels came from, and that harm may be harder to specify by prompting than by finetuning on labelled examples. So never select a filter, judge or guard model on an offline score alone when you can afford one end-to-end run; the offline score is for narrowing the candidate set, not for choosing from it.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: it is not that prompted classifiers are worse than finetuned ones in general, and not that F1 is the wrong metric. It is that this particular offline metric, computed on labels generated by prompting LLMs, ranked two candidates in the opposite order from the deployment objective. The sibling case in this corpus is naively generated eval queries that do not resemble real user queries ([[089c7cba]]); the same label-provenance problem shows up wherever a measurement's ground truth was synthesised by the system it is measuring.

TWO OTHER THINGS THE SOURCE ESTABLISHES AND BOUNDS. The threshold on the chosen classifier is a tunable safety-usefulness tradeoff, and natural science is the benign area that pays for aggressive filtering. And the robustness question is open by the authors' own statement: they did not test whether adversarial finetuning recovers the removed capabilities, so filtering is not established as resistant to later fine-tuning. The post also proposes extending the method to misalignment-relevant documents; that is proposed, not tested.
