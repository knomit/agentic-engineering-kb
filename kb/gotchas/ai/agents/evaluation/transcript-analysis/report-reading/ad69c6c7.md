# Three read-traps in an agentic trajectory report: a sub-agent's label describes the delegation prompt not its behaviour, the timeline axis is turn order not elapsed time, and a file read is not an influence

UK AISI's Transect (2026-10-07) renders a long agent run as a timeline of labelled activity, and the post names three ways a reader draws a false conclusion from it. Each caveat is stated by the tool's own authors about their own output, which is what makes them worth holding: they are not criticisms of the tool, they are the correct way to read it.

**1. A sub-agent's classification is a claim about the orchestrator, not about the sub-agent.** Transect classifies sub-agents from their delegation instructions, and the post says verbatim: "these labels describe what they were asked to do, not necessarily what they did." So a report showing "3 sub-agents doing data analysis" is evidence that three sub-agents were *asked* to do data analysis. In a multi-agent trajectory the delegation prompt is the cheapest thing to label and the easiest to mistake for an outcome; verifying what a sub-agent did means reading its own transcript.

**2. The horizontal axis is log order, not time.** The timeline is indexed by turns — verbatim, "Each turn is one recorded agent output, which may contain text, tool calls or both" — and in the post's own multi-agent figure, horizontal position follows log order rather than elapsed time, and bars do not establish continuous or simultaneous activity. Two bars overlapping is therefore NOT evidence of concurrency, and a long bar is NOT evidence of a long-running step. Anything you want to say about wall-clock duration or parallelism needs timestamps the turn index does not carry.

**3. Access is not influence.** Verbatim: "An agent reading a file does not necessarily mean it influenced its later behaviour." A shared-file or shared-artefact analysis gives you a read/write graph; attributing a downstream decision to an upstream read is an additional claim requiring additional evidence.

**Why these traps exist at all, and the scale that forces the tool.** Verbatim: "evaluation transcripts can run to billions of tokens, exceeding what human experts can review in full." At that scale nobody reads the transcript and everybody reads the rendered summary, so a misread of the rendering becomes the finding of record. The post's own counter-discipline is that every labelled stretch links back to its transcript passage — use those links before quoting a label as a result.

**Scope.** All three caveats come from a single source describing one tool, and the multi-agent figure is a custom case study of ONE run (an agent given six calendar days, substantial compute, and the ability to delegate), not a validated benchmark. The post reports no accuracy figures for the labelling. The generalisation beyond Transect is the pack's: any turn-indexed trajectory visualisation of a delegating agent has the same three traps, because they come from what a turn index and a delegation prompt can and cannot tell you.
