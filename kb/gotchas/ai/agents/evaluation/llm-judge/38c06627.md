# LLM judges have position, length and self-preference biases you must actively cancel

When scoring with an LLM judge, prefer pairwise comparison of two outputs over asking for a single-output Likert score — relative judgements are far more stable than absolute ones. But pairwise introduces its own artefacts that will silently inflate your numbers unless you cancel them:

- Position bias: the judge favours one slot regardless of content. Cancel it by running each pair twice with the order reversed and only counting consistent verdicts.
- Length bias: judges prefer longer responses. Control for it explicitly, or you will "improve" your system by making it more verbose.
- Self-preference bias: a judge scores its own family's outputs higher. Verbatim from the source, reporting the Vicuna/MT-Bench work: "GPT-4 favors itself with a 10% higher win rate while Claude-v1 favors itself with a 25% higher win rate." The operational consequence is specific: never let the model you are shipping also be the judge that decides it improved, and be suspicious of any A/B where the judge shares a family with one arm.
- Allow ties. Forcing a winner injects noise on genuinely equivalent pairs.
- Ask for chain-of-thought reasoning before the verdict, not after.

On how good a judge can get, verbatim: "Under the single answer grading paradigm, GPT-4 had higher agreement with humans (85%) than the humans had amongst themselves (81%)." A well-built judge can sit at roughly the human-to-human ceiling, which is the honest bar to compare against rather than 100%. Separately reported correlations are more sobering: Spearman 0.514 with human judgement on coherence in news summarization (G-Eval), and "Spearman rank correlation of 0.55 at the model level" on Vicuna-benchmark rankings (QLoRA) — note that 0.55 is a MODEL-LEVEL ranking correlation, not per-response agreement, so it does not license claims about individual verdicts.

The applied-llms authors report "agreement with humans and LLM improved from 68% to 94%" across three iterations of judge refinement — the useful implication being that an unrefined judge sitting at ~68% agreement looks perfectly plausible in a dashboard while being wrong about a third of the time.

Related trap: over-optimising a single metric degrades others. applied-llms state that "an overemphasis on NIAH evals can reduce performance on extraction and summarization tasks" — note the hedge, "can reduce", a claimed tendency with no measurement attached — and give the mechanism: "Because these LLMs are so finetuned to attend to every sentence, they may start to treat irrelevant details and distractors as important, thus including them in the final output." The mechanism is the actionable part. Evaluate on realistic tasks, not synthetic recall probes.

WHICH REF SUPPORTS WHICH FIGURE, because the two are easy to confuse and a check pointed at the wrong one confirms nothing. The self-preference win rates, the 85%-vs-81% agreement figure and both Spearman correlations are in eugeneyan's llm-patterns, which cites the Vicuna/MT-Bench, G-Eval and QLoRA papers for them; they are NOT in applied-llms. Only the 68%-to-94% progression and the NIAH trap are applied-llms'.

Caveat on the numbers: the self-preference and agreement figures come from 2023-era evaluations (Claude-v1, GPT-4) and the exact magnitudes should not be assumed to carry over to current models. The direction of the bias is the durable part; re-measure the size on whatever judge you actually deploy.
