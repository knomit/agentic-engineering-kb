# A judge model matched human raters MORE often than humans matched each other — 59% model-human exact agreement against 35% human-human, so the calibration target is inter-rater agreement, not correctness

Building a prototype index of how automated its own AI R&D is, Anthropic validated the Claude judge that assigned automation levels by having the staff who own each work area rate it too, blind: "staff made their ratings without knowing what evidence the models had gathered or how they had judged that evidence." The result: "Our judge model agreed with humans about as often as humans agreed with each other (model-versus-human exact agreement was 59%, human-versus-human was 35%), and model and human ratings were within one level of each other 97% of the time."

THE TRANSFERABLE POINT, and it is a correction to how most judge validations are read. A raw model-human agreement figure is uninterpretable on its own, because the implicit reference — what a human would have said — is itself noisy, and on a six-level ordinal scale it was noisier than the model. 59% sounds like a weak judge until the human-human 35% is next to it. So the honest calibration target is the inter-rater agreement of the humans you are replacing, measured on the same items; a judge that clears it is at the ceiling the task allows, and pushing past that measures agreement with a particular rater rather than accuracy. Report both numbers or neither, and report the within-one-level figure separately for an ordinal scale, because exact-match agreement understates a judge whose errors are all adjacent.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN. It is not evidence the judge is correct — both raters can be wrong together, and Anthropic names the specific version of that risk in this setup: "we're using our own models to evaluate our systems, which could mean that the 'judge' model could make the same kinds of errors as the model it is checking." Its proposed remedy is third-party verification or other developers' models, with guardrails on competitively sensitive data. Nor does a good agreement figure say anything about the failure in [[fa5bc47a]], where judges correctly identify what happened and assign the other label because of what the label will be used for; agreement studies on a neutral rating task do not probe that.

THE INDEX ITSELF, for the numbers. Using Epoch AI's Automation Level scale (AL0 no AI involvement through AL5 fully autonomous, with AL3 "AI collaborates" and AL4 "AI leads"), as of August 2026 Anthropic reports that Claude is NOT operating fully autonomously for any measured subset of AI R&D work, that Claude "leads" 26% of Anthropic's AI R&D work, and that the share at or above "AI collaborates" is above 90%. Method: a 20% weekly random sample of staff in each department in the model R&D loop for each week of July 2026, a Claude agent reading each sampled person's week from Slack and internal documentation, yielding ~15,000 granular tasks organised into a frozen 542-node tree with 378 leaves, weighted by person-time rather than by an editorial judgement of importance.

SCOPE, stated by the operator. The basket is FROZEN on a July 2026 baseline, so a rising index says the work humans were doing then is being automated — it does not by itself say whether humans have moved onto new work. Anthropic tested this by building an alternate tree from January 2026 data and comparing new tasks arriving February-July against it, finding "no rise in the number of 'novel' tasks", and plans to rebuild the basket periodically. Rating agents saw only evidence from the month being rated or earlier. The person-time weighting is called "a crude approximation" by its authors. These are self-reported figures about one company, not a cross-lab comparison — Anthropic names the absence of a common methodology as the first of two obstacles to that.
