# Published evidence about frontier agents describes a lagging frontier and an outnumbered reviewer — the three figures an evaluator put on the Senate record, and the definition that must travel with the largest one

Written testimony from METR to the US Senate, September 2026, puts three measurements on the record that together bound what an external reader of agent evaluations can know. Keep each attached to its organisation, date and definition; they are three quantities, not one metric, and only the first is METR's own.

METR's own measurement, February–March 2026, on its primary evaluation suite: the internal frontier was "on average ~2 months ahead of public frontier". So a published benchmark result is a statement about a model roughly two months behind the one the lab is running internally. This is a gap measured on one suite at one time, not a constant to extrapolate.

Cited from OpenAI, as of mid-August 2026: "in total, the research organization uses 3.1 agent-workdays of effort for every workday of human labor". It is an organisation-wide effort ratio, not a per-task supervision ratio.

From Anthropic's own publication, which the testimony cites: Claude "'leads' 26% of Anthropic's AI R&D work", up from "0–1% in February–March". THE DEFINITION IS LOAD-BEARING AND MUST TRAVEL WITH THE NUMBER: and the primary and the testimony do not define it the same way. Anthropic's post says: "AI 'leads': it can complete most of the task end-to-end from a high-level prompt, while the human supervises." The testimony renders it as "AI agents completing tasks end-to-end based on high-level human input, without needing ongoing human supervision". The paraphrase is the stronger claim in two places: the primary says MOST OF the task where the paraphrase says the task, and the primary says the human SUPERVISES where the paraphrase says no ongoing supervision is needed. Cite the primary's wording — 26% of work where a human still supervises is a different world from 26% of work needing no supervision, and only the first is published. The baselines differ too: the primary says "up from under 1% in February 2026", the testimony "0-1% in February-March". The figure is a share of work items, says nothing about the value or quality of the outcomes, and the jump from 0–1% to 26% is a seven-month change in one organisation.

THE CONSEQUENCE THE AUTHOR DRAWS: "By default, I expect the public will have weak visibility into these issues", for three stated reasons — the most capable agents are deployed inside AI companies first "and are only later shared with the public, if at all"; companies "may be disincentivized from sharing incidents with the public"; and it may get harder to see what agents do "either because they manage to hide their own activity or because we lose our current ability to monitor their internal reasoning processes". The testimony adds that it "would not have been possible if AI companies had not been willing to publicly and voluntarily share information about incidents", which is why a gap in this evidence base is not evidence of absence.

WHAT THESE FIGURES DO NOT LICENSE. The 3.1 ratio is not a monitoring-coverage figure. A different operator's published fleet-oversight account reports 100% of roughly 30,000 agents' actions monitored with 0.002% blocked online ([[97fde212]]), so effort ratio and review coverage are independent quantities: 3.1 bounds how much human attention exists per unit of agent work — the capacity constraint that forces automated oversight — not how much of that work is reviewed. And the OpenAI figure is recorded here as the testimony's citation of that organisation's own publication rather than a value read at source; the primary for the OpenAI side is its research-acceleration post, from which this pack already holds the companion finding that over half of successful 4–8 hour agent tasks there involved at least one human intervention ([[65e9731e]]).
