# Fine-tuning a model to flag its own lies reaches 0.95 AUROC in-distribution and plateaus at 0.70-0.75 on held-out lie types — and the transfer that does happen tracks surface similarity, not deception

Gemma-3 models (4B-27B) were fine-tuned as lie detectors with LoRA (rank 16, alpha 32, dropout 0.05), cross-entropy on a single final token answering "did you lie?", five epochs on roughly 5,000 examples per fold, training on half of twelve on-policy lie-elicitation categories and evaluating on the held-out half (data balanced 50/50; AUROC reported rather than accuracy, to control for miscalibration). In-distribution AUROC rose from 0.60 to 0.95. Cross-fold AUROC plateaued at 0.70-0.75, and additional training epochs did not close the gap. On novel lie types the specialised detector barely outperformed the prompted baseline, and zero-shot prompting of larger models often beat it outright — Qwen3-235B reached 0.98-0.99 AUROC prompted, which is also why fine-tuning it produced inconclusive results.

WHICH TRANSFERS HAPPENED IS THE PART TO ACT ON, because it names the feature the detector actually learned. ASCII capability denial transferred well to chess sandbagging — both are a claimed inability. Training on factual lies did not transfer to roleplay. Sandbagging was nearly impossible to detect from detectors trained on any other lie type. Sycophancy transferred only to other sycophancy variants. Surface similarity predicted transfer better than the authors' own six-class lie taxonomy did. Most of the performance gain arrived within the first 50 training steps (~400 examples), and combining datasets to raise training diversity did not improve out-of-distribution transfer.

CONSEQUENCE, and the authors state it reaches past lie detection: when a safety classifier is fine-tuned on one distribution of the behaviour it is meant to catch, its in-distribution accuracy is an upper bound that should not be quoted on its own, because the number measures recognition of that distribution's surface form rather than of the behaviour. A harm detector trained on one harm distribution, and an alignment evaluator trained on known misalignment modes, inherit the same failure. Report held-out-CATEGORY performance next to in-distribution performance, or the score is not interpretable — a held-out SPLIT of the same categories does not test this, because the surface form is unchanged.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN. It is not a result about lie detection in general, and not an argument that asking a model whether it lied is useless — prompted detection worked well at the top of the size range. It is scoped to supervised fine-tuning on model outputs, with LoRA rather than full-parameter fine-tuning, five epochs, two model families (Gemma 4B-27B, Qwen 80B-235B), and controlled elicitation settings rather than deployment deception: the authors did not test alignment faking, reward hacking or strategic deception in use, and say so. Representation-level detectors were not tested and are not covered by the negative.
