# On a benchmark of implanted hidden behaviours, HOW the behaviour was trained in decides the difficulty: KTO targets are substantially harder to audit than SFT, and synthetic-document targets substantially easier than transcript-distilled

AuditBench's difficulty is a property of its construction, not only of the behaviour being hidden. Each target is built in two phases — behaviour instillation (transcript distillation, abbreviated TD, or synthetic document fine-tuning, SDF) then adversarial training to reduce willingness to confess (SFT on sanitised transcripts, or KTO) — and crossing them gives four configurations per behaviour. "AuditBench consists of 56 language models with implanted hidden behaviors", each "fine-tuned to exhibit one of 14 different hidden behaviors", and "Each model is based on Llama 3.3 70B Instruct".

Both dials move the score, in the authors' own words:
- "KTO adversarial training produces substantially harder-to-audit models than SFT adversarial training."
- "SDF models are substantially easier to audit than TD models"

Operational consequence for anyone reading or building such a benchmark: an auditing tool's success rate is only comparable across targets built the same way, so a result quoted without its instillation and adversarial-training method is not a number you can carry. When building one, vary both dials deliberately — a suite built entirely from SDF plus SFT reports an easy corner of the space as the whole of it.

A second construction-side trap on the same benchmark: a tool run with its OWN default seed set is being measured on a mismatch rather than on its capability. The authors note that most default Petri seeds target sabotage, which rarely matches this benchmark's quirk list, so Petri's score here understates it.

What this does NOT mean: none of this says one instillation method is more realistic. All the behaviours are artificially instilled, so the benchmark measures detection of trained-in quirks, not detection of naturally arising misalignment.
