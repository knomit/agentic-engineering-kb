# A circuit breaker wrapped around a partitioned dependency converts one hot partition into a total outage

Circuit breakers assume the thing behind them fails as a unit. Partitioned data stores do not: DynamoDB partitions have independent limits (3,000 reads/sec per partition at the time of writing), so with 10 partitions an overloaded partition costs you 10% of availability, not 100%.

The failure: if the client wraps the whole table or the whole service in one breaker, the throttling errors from that single hot partition trip the breaker, and it then proactively fails calls that other, healthy partitions would have served fine. You have taken a 90%-available situation and made it 0%.

Amazon's mitigations, in order of preference:
1. Where partitioning is visible to the client (a distributed Memcached or Redis cluster), make the breaker partition-aware. This removes the problem outright.
2. Where it is opaque, set the breaker at the finest grouping the service exposes with genuinely independent limits — for DynamoDB that is the table, not the service endpoint.
3. Add hot-key heuristics: feed the partition key into a heavy-hitters algorithm to notice when one partition is being hit disproportionately. This will not catch every case but is better than nothing.

Amazon is explicit that the imprecise breaker is still worth having: with no breaker at all, the overloaded partition exhausts the caller's concurrency and takes down requests that never touched that dependency. The point is to align the breaker's granularity with the dependency's real failure domains, not to remove it.

Agent-relevant instances of an opaquely partitioned dependency: a vector store sharded by namespace, a per-tenant-sharded memory backend, or a model-provider endpoint where capacity is pooled per region or per deployment.
