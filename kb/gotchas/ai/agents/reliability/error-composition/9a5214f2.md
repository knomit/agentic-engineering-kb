# As reasoning and action sequences lengthen, the errors that remain shift from systematic to random — and raising an explicit reasoning budget does not buy back what spontaneous overthinking costs

The post splits error into bias (consistently, reliably wrong) and variance (unpredictable across samples) and defines a ratio over them: "We define error incoherence as the fraction of error attributable to variance". Near zero, the remaining errors are systematic and can be chased to a cause; near one, they are scatter.

Three findings, each with the condition that bounds it.

First, horizon length moves the composition in one direction: "Across all tasks and models, the longer models spend reasoning and taking actions, the more incoherent their errors become." It held in the post's synthetic optimiser arm as well as on benchmarks.

Second, scale does not reliably fix this, and the direction depends on task difficulty: "On benchmark tasks, more intelligent models made more coherent errors on easy tasks, while their errors on the hardest tasks became more incoherent or remained the same." So a result measured on easy tasks does not transfer to hard ones, and in the synthetic arm scale cut bias faster than it cut variance.

Third, and this is the asymmetry to design around, the post's own finding heading: "Natural \"overthinking\" increases incoherence in errors more than reasoning budgets reduce it." Setting a reasoning budget is therefore not a remedy for spontaneous overthinking — the cause is stronger than the control.

The operational consequence for a long-horizon agent is that debugging effort should follow the composition, not the rate. Where the remaining error is mostly variance, chasing a goal-specification or prompt bug is chasing something that is not there, and sampling several times and reconciling is what pays. The post names the limit on that remedy directly: ensembling reduces variance but is impractical for irreversible real-world actions — which is exactly the class of action where you most want it. Treat irreversibility, not cost, as the thing that rules ensembling out.

What this does NOT mean: total error does not rise with reasoning length. The framework deliberately measures the COMPOSITION of error rather than its frequency, so that falling total error from scaling cannot hide what the remaining errors look like. A model can get better and have its residual failures get less diagnosable at the same time.

Two scope limits. The decomposition needs a well-defined target to measure against — an answer choice, a unit test, an objective function — so it cannot be applied to open-ended goals or hidden objectives, and complex incoherent behaviour in natural settings is left as an open problem. And the post's framing is about what failures will LOOK like (closer to industrial accidents than to coherent pursuit of the wrong goal), which it offers as a reason to weight reward hacking and goal misspecification — the bias term — more heavily in research, not as evidence that goal misspecification is rare.
