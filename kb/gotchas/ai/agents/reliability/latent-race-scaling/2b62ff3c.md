# A race with a one-instruction window produced a crash every few hours once three independent rates rose together — so code that has never failed in eighteen years is not evidence that it is safe at your load

The defect was a race in GNU libunwind, present since the first x86_64 version with C++ unwinding. During exception cleanup the library "hands a pointer to that struct to an internal assembly routine: _Ux86_64_setcontext", which moves the stack pointer before reading the target instruction pointer out of a stack-allocated ucontext_t. A SIGUSR2 arriving inside that window lets the kernel write its signal frame over the struct, leaving a NULL instruction pointer and a return-to-null crash.

What makes this a reliability lesson rather than a C++ anecdote is why it appeared when it did. The crash probability is a product of three rates that rose independently: how often the process throws (Rockset throws frequently for backpressure, and an overloaded host can reach roughly 10^4 exceptions per second), how often the signal fires (SIGUSR2 roughly every 10^-2 seconds of CPU time against a vulnerable window of about one instruction, so order 10^-8 per cleanup), and how much stack the signal handler uses — and that last one is what finally crossed the threshold: "earlier this year we made the SIGUSR2 handler use more stack by adding a call to timer_getoverrun". Previously the 128-byte red zone had absorbed the overwrite. Multiply the rates and a 10^-8 event becomes a crash every few hours on a loaded host.

The operational consequence: when a rare failure appears without a code change to the failing component, look for an unrelated change that raised one factor in a product, and look at the load the component is now under rather than at its track record. The fix here was to stop using the component in that path — switching to libgcc's unwinder — while also upstreaming a fix, because the upstream fix does not reach a running fleet.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: the figures above are the source's own order-of-magnitude reasoning about why the bug surfaced now, not measured crash rates; the one measured fleet rate is the dozen-plus return-to-null crashes per day. It also does not mean age makes a dependency suspect — the point is the opposite of a heuristic about old code: the age of the defect carried no signal at all, and treating "this has worked for years" as evidence is what let the assumption stand.
