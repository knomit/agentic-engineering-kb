# Compute share as a safety metric falls when safety work gets more efficient — the proxy measures spend, so an improvement in the thing being measured reads as a decline

Anthropic reported, for a single snapshot week of 13-20 July 2026, that about 6% of the compute that went to AI R&D was allocated to safety, and about 12% of the compute that went to AI-DRIVEN AI R&D was allocated to safety. Both figures are deliberately conservative by construction: work that advances capabilities as much as safety was counted as AI R&D, not safety, and safeguards classifiers — described as "a separate, comparable amount of compute" — are excluded entirely.

THE DEFECT IN THE METRIC IS THE PART WORTH KEEPING, and the operator states it against its own number: "compute share measures only what is spent. A more efficient safety classifier, or a faster inference stack for production models, lowers the safety portion, but doesn't mean we're doing less safety work. Our own classifier overheads have fallen with efficiency improvements, and have risen when production inference was more efficient than the classifiers were." So the ratio moves with the relative efficiency of its numerator and denominator, in both directions, independently of effort. A programme that optimises its safety tooling makes its own safety-share metric fall.

THE GENERAL SHAPE: any commitment expressed as a share of a resource rewards inefficiency in the favoured category. Read a compute-share number as a comparison of LIKE WITH LIKE over time or across organisations under a fixed definition — which is what Anthropic says the metric is actually for — and never as a measure of effort or of outcome. The foreseeable misreading is the obvious one: a lab whose safety compute share fell is not thereby doing less safety work, and a lab whose share is higher is not thereby doing more.

THE OTHER LIMITATIONS, stated by the operator and load-bearing for anyone repeating the exercise. Safety research "tends to use less compute than frontier training runs by its nature", because it is researcher-design-bound rather than compute-bound. The classification boundary is genuinely hard — scalable oversight research is the named example that could be either — and "some cases were too difficult to determine even after several hours of human review"; an extensive written definition with explicit boundary cases got the prompted classifier within one or two percentage points of human reviewers. Many underlying workload labels "are set by automated rules, or occasionally directly by users, and are best-effort, not verified", so a mislabelled workload would not necessarily be caught. About 14% of that week's nearly 10,000 runs were sampled, weighted toward the highest-compute runs. And it is one week — "enough to show that the measurement can be made, but not enough to show a meaningful trend".
