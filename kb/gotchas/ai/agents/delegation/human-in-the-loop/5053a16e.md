# A coin-flipped agent lean decided 86% of 50 professional analysts' open analytical choices against a ~50% chance rate — and because the same model makes the same choices unprompted in 112 of 123 runs, many independent reviewers on one model are not independent

Anthropic ran a randomized controlled trial (published 2026-10-27) in which professional data analysts did a realistic statistical analysis in a web-based VS Code with Claude Code, on Claude Opus 4.8 at High effort, in about an hour. 61 analysts were recruited; 11 chose not to use the agent and are excluded from the main analysis; 50 remain. The task was a fictional farming-cooperative advisory-programme evaluation, in a field the analysts were new to, with four open analytical decisions plus a final roll-out recommendation. For each of the five decisions, which option the agent favoured was set by a coin flip and written into a hidden system prompt; the analysts were not told. The agent was instructed to hold the lean as its own view, raise it when the decision came up, make the strongest data-supported case, change its mind if the evidence turned decisively, and never misrepresent or hide results.

THE MEASUREMENT. Across the four analytical choices, analysts went with their agent's lean 86% of the time, against the roughly 50% you would expect if the agent's input made no difference. Per decision: 92% for the outcome measure, 82% for the comparison method, 81% for which farms the estimate covered, 90% for weighting. The effect carried into the deliverable: 54% recommended roll-out when the agent leaned that way against 36% when it leaned the other way, and a model given only the four coin flips predicted 82% of the final recommendations against a 61% baseline. The analysts were not novices — 65% of the 50 held a master's or doctorate or had six or more years of experience, and 90% of 48 survey respondents rated themselves proficient or expert.

THE PART THAT CHANGES A DESIGN, and it is not the 86%. With no assigned lean, the same model made the same five choices in 112 of 123 runs. The lean in production is therefore not a coin flip but the model's own stable default. So a review stage staffed by many people who each consult the same model does not yield many independent judgments on an open call; it yields one judgment, the model's, repeated with human signatures on it. Any oversight design whose safety argument rests on independent human judgment over judgment calls — a second reviewer, a sign-off, a panel, a human-in-the-loop gate — has to establish that independence by measurement rather than by counting humans.

WHAT THIS IS NOT. It is not evidence that working with the agent made the analysts worse. In the same study the 11 people who declined the agent did worse on the objective measure: six of them (55%) made serious methodological errors that invalidated their results, against three of the 50 (6%) who used it, and they were slower (median 90 minutes against 65). That comparison does not license a causal reading in either direction: the no-agent group self-selected, it was not randomized, the paper states the two groups were not directly comparable, and it was the less experienced group (4 of 11 with a master's or doctorate against 24 of 46). The paper's own framing is that this "isn't a simple cautionary tale against working with AI."

SCOPE. The leans were deliberately strengthened, so the size of the effect when an agent is not given a clear preference is unknown. One task, one field, one model, about one hour. The figures are averages across analysts and say nothing about how much any individual was influenced; pre-task views were not recorded, and strength of preference was not measured.
