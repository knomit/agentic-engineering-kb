# When the author is an agent, reviewer noise is not an annoyance but a cost multiplier — every nit is another commit and another round, and one PR ran more than fifty rounds overnight before a human stopped it

The Claude Code team's account of its own review pipeline names a failure that only appears once BOTH sides of review are automated: with a human author, a low-value review comment costs one person a minute of judgement and is ignored; with an agent author, it costs a fix commit, a re-review and another round, and nothing in the loop gets tired. "One CLI PR went through more than fifty rounds of review and fixes overnight before its author stopped it." Agent authors and agent reviewers can keep each other busy indefinitely.

So the team optimizes CONVERGENCE as a first-class metric alongside bug-finding, and the measures are mechanical:
- A round cap: "After three rounds, the reviewer asks the author to step back and reconsider the approach."
- Re-review restricted to the diff after the first few passes, rather than the whole change again.
- Flagging the bug CLASS so every instance is fixed at once rather than one per round.
- Never repeating a finding already posted, and clearing a finding automatically when a later commit fixes it.
- Dropping style, diagnostic and cleanup-only findings outright.

The diagnosis behind the diff-only rule is worth carrying on its own, because it is the non-obvious part: "about 59% of it was code that had been there since the reviewer's first pass." The later rounds were not converging on the change under review; they were re-mining the surrounding file. A reviewer with whole-repo context re-earns its own backlog on every pass unless something narrows its scope as the rounds go on.

Results are given as a before/after figure for "the convergence work", read off the chart's own labels rather than prose: pass after one review-and-fix round 65% to 78%; need five or more rounds 8.9% to 1.5%; mean reviews to pass 2.08 to 1.32. Over the same programme the team reports merged PRs to Claude Code more than doubling from June to September while the share reverted fell by more than 40%.

What this does NOT mean. It is not an argument for fewer review passes in general: the stated conclusion is that several rounds per PR are worthwhile for RISKY changes and were being spent by habit everywhere else, and the team explicitly rejects slowing down as the remedy because it reduces errors mostly by doing less. Nor is the round cap a safety control — the same account says there are no circuit breakers that tighten approval automatically when revert or incident rates rise, and detection still rests on the author, on-call and dashboards. Precision and recall for the reviewer are not published, so the convergence numbers say nothing about whether the findings were right.
