# An "encrypted" reasoning trace is not a secret — the ciphertext is not broken, the model that can read it is simply asked to transcribe it, and it replays across accounts

Frontier APIs return the model's reasoning as an encrypted blob that the client stores and passes back: "AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic send reasoning traces back and forth as part of their messaging protocols." Because the blob is opaque to the client, harnesses persist it in ordinary session files — "For Codex, session files are stored under directories such as: ~/.codex/sessions/2026/08/14" — and those files get shared, committed and attached to bug reports as if they carried nothing readable. Published 2026-08-16.

THE ARCHITECTURAL POINT, WHICH IS SHARPER THAN THE EXPLOIT. Nobody breaks the ciphertext. The blob is replayed into a model on the same provider that CAN decrypt it, and that model is then talked into reading its contents out with an injection along the lines of asking it to transcribe the thought. So the confidentiality of the trace is enforced by the receiving model's willingness to refuse, not by cryptography — and a model's willingness to refuse is the one control this pack has repeatedly documented failing. Any design that treats a provider-encrypted field as a security boundary has picked a boundary made of model behaviour.

THE CROSS-ACCOUNT DEMONSTRATION, in the author's own words: "I created a reasoning trace containing a known password using GPT-5.6 Sol under one OpenAI account. I then attempted to recover that trace using GPT-5.6 Luna from a different account", and "I was able to recover reasoning content from OpenAI traces across separate sessions and accounts, including a password." A DIFFERENT model, a DIFFERENT account, and a weaker one at that — so possession of the blob is closer to sufficient than tenancy suggests.

THE PROPOSED CAUSE IS THE AUTHOR'S HYPOTHESIS AND IS HEDGED — DO NOT HARDEN IT: "This seems possible because providers likely use shared encryption keys across users, sessions, and models." "Likely" is the source's word. The cross-account recovery is demonstrated; the key-sharing explanation for it is inferred, and no provider is quoted confirming it.

SCALE, AND WHOSE MEASUREMENT IT IS. The large numbers are NOT the author's. He reports them from a cited paper, "Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs": "They decoded 315,320 reasoning blocks scraped from public repositories and recovered 367 pieces of PII and 182 credentials, including API keys and passwords." Note what that population is — blobs scraped from PUBLIC REPOSITORIES, i.e. session files people had already committed. The recovery rate against a targeted single victim is not established by it.

WHY THE ENCRYPTION EXISTS AT ALL, which explains why this is unlikely to be fixed the way a user would want: the stated purposes are "Preventing model behavior cloning and distillation; Protecting proprietary model behavior; Limiting information leakage from internal reasoning". The first two are the vendor's interest, not yours. The encryption was never primarily a user-confidentiality control, so do not expect it to be hardened as one.

WHAT TO DO, and the author's own framing: "Encrypted reasoning blobs should not be treated as harmless opaque data. If you share session files containing them, you may be sharing significantly more information than you realize." Concretely, treat an agent session file as containing cleartext secrets for every purpose that matters — retention policy, repository hygiene, support-bundle redaction, and what you attach to a ticket. THIS PACK'S ADDITION, marked as inference: the failure is worse than a transcript leak, because reasoning is exactly where a model restates credentials, internal hostnames and intermediate conclusions that never appear in the visible output, so the encrypted field is systematically the MOST sensitive part of the record and the part everyone assumes is inert.

NOT ESTABLISHED: the post does not state that any vendor was notified or responded, and does not say whether the behaviour has since changed. Confidence is held at 0.75 on that basis — a single practitioner post, with the central causal claim hedged by its own author and the largest figures belonging to a paper this pack has not read.
