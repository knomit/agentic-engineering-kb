# An "encrypted" reasoning trace is not a secret — the ciphertext is not broken, the model that can read it is simply asked to transcribe it, and it replays across accounts

Frontier APIs return the model's reasoning as an encrypted blob that the client stores and passes back: "AI labs like OpenAI and Anthropic send reasoning traces back and forth as part of their messaging protocols." Because the blob is opaque to the client, harnesses persist it in ordinary session files — "For Codex, session files are stored under directories such as: ~/.codex/sessions/2026/08/14" — and those files get shared, committed and attached to bug reports as if they carried nothing readable. Published 2026-08-16.

THE ARCHITECTURAL POINT, WHICH IS SHARPER THAN THE EXPLOIT. Nobody breaks the ciphertext. The blob is replayed into a model on the same provider that CAN decrypt it, and that model is then talked into reading its contents out with an injection along the lines of asking it to transcribe the thought. So the confidentiality of the trace is enforced by the receiving model's willingness to refuse, not by cryptography — and a model's willingness to refuse is the one control this pack has repeatedly documented failing. Any design that treats a provider-encrypted field as a security boundary has picked a boundary made of model behaviour. The generalisation of this to any opaque client-held handle is in [[33aed84c]].

THE CROSS-ACCOUNT DEMONSTRATION, in the author's own words: "I created a reasoning trace containing a known password using GPT-5.6 Sol under one OpenAI account. I then attempted to recover that trace using GPT-5.6 Luna from a different account", and "I was able to recover reasoning content from OpenAI traces across separate sessions and accounts, including a password." A DIFFERENT model, a DIFFERENT account, and a weaker one at that — so possession of the blob is closer to sufficient than tenancy suggests.

*** THE CAUSAL CLAIM IS NO LONGER ONLY THE BLOG AUTHOR'S HYPOTHESIS. *** The fact previously flagged "This seems possible because providers likely use shared encryption keys across users, sessions, and models" as an unsupported inference. The cited paper, now read, reaches the same conclusion from systematic testing "against major API vendors including OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic": "providers appear to be using a single global key to encrypt and authenticate every reasoning block." It measures the consequence directly as three named compatibility forms — in- and cross-session, cross-user, and cross-model. NOTE THAT BOTH SOURCES STILL HEDGE ("likely", "appear to be"): no provider is quoted confirming key management, so the mechanism remains inferred from behaviour, but it is now inferred independently by two parties from different evidence. Confidence raised 0.75 -> 0.85 on that basis.

SCALE, AND WHAT IT IS AND IS NOT A RATE OF. The large numbers are the paper's, not the blog author's: "By decoding 315,320 reasoning blocks scraped from public repositories, we recovered 367 Personally Identifiable Information (PII) artifacts and 182 credentials." The paper separately states "We collected 6,708 publicly available agent trajectories from GitHub and Hugging Face."
CORRECTED BY THIS RUN'S SELF-REVIEW: an earlier version of this paragraph combined those two sentences into "~6.7k public trajectories yielding ~315k blocks — roughly 47 blocks per trajectory". The paper does not say the 315,320 blocks came from exactly those 6,708 trajectories, so that division asserted a relationship neither sentence makes. Removed. This is the pack's own sub-rule against joining two adjacent source claims, caught in a fact written the same run the rule was applied to someone else's work.
WHAT DOES SURVIVE, and it is the part that matters: the population is PUBLIC material — blobs in repositories people had already committed — so 367 and 182 are hits across a large public corpus, not a recovery rate against a targeted victim. What this establishes is that the leak is real and already at scale in the open; it establishes nothing about your odds if someone comes after your session files specifically.

*** VENDOR RESPONSE: THE GAP IS CLOSED, AND THE ANSWER IS PARTIAL RATHER THAN REASSURING. *** This fact previously said the post "does not state that any vendor was notified or responded, and does not say whether the behaviour has since changed". The paper answers both: disclosure happened, and "As of August 2026, the results presented in Figure 1 are no longer reproducible with attacks described in Section 2.4 and Appendix C because of mitigations implemented by providers following our disclosure."
READ THE SCOPE OF THAT SENTENCE CAREFULLY, because it is narrower than it first sounds. It says one figure's results, obtained by two named sections' attacks, no longer reproduce. It does NOT say the architecture changed, that client-side traces are gone, or that the class is closed. AND THE ORDERING CUTS AGAINST THE COMFORTABLE READING: the blog post is dated 2026-08-16 and CITES this paper, so it post-dates it — and it reports a successful cross-account password recovery using a plain transcription request rather than the paper's extraction machinery. THESE TWO ARE NOT IN CONTRADICTION and no `decisions` fact is warranted: they describe different attacks, and the paper's claim is explicitly scoped to its own. The honest synthesis is that specific extraction techniques were patched and the underlying replayability was still exploitable by simpler means at the latest date either source covers. Do not upgrade this to "fixed".

WHY THE ENCRYPTION EXISTS AT ALL, which explains why this is unlikely to be fixed the way a user would want: the stated purposes are "Preventing model behavior cloning and distillation; Protecting proprietary model behavior; Limiting information leakage from internal reasoning". The first two are the vendor's interest, not yours. The encryption was never primarily a user-confidentiality control, so do not expect it to be hardened as one. The paper's own mitigation list is consistent with this — its only structural fix is to stop shipping traces to clients at all, which costs the providers the statelessness the design was chosen for.

WHAT TO DO, and the author's own framing: "Encrypted reasoning blobs should not be treated as harmless opaque data. If you share session files containing them, you may be sharing significantly more information than you realize." Concretely, treat an agent session file as containing cleartext secrets for every purpose that matters — retention policy, repository hygiene, support-bundle redaction, and what you attach to a ticket. THIS PACK'S ADDITION, marked as inference: the failure is worse than a transcript leak, because reasoning is exactly where a model restates credentials, internal hostnames and intermediate conclusions that never appear in the visible output, so the encrypted field is systematically the MOST sensitive part of the record and the part everyone assumes is inert.

THE FLOW RUNS BOTH WAYS. This fact covers secrets leaking OUT of a reasoning blob. The same replayability carries instructions IN — an injection sealed inside a block is received as the model's own prior reasoning, invisible to any text-reading monitor. That is [[a7863d24]], and it is the more serious of the two for anyone building agents rather than handling session files.
