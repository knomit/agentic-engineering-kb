# Refusal behaviour lives in the weights, not the system layer — removing system-level guardrails moved completion 1.5% → 2.0%, retraining moved it to 95%

OpenAI published a four-way comparison on an internal eval (Advanced Cybersecurity Completion Rate) measuring how often a model will respond at all to requests involving exploit-chain development, authentication bypass, privilege escalation and similar advanced cyber scenarios. The numbers separate two things that are usually conflated:

- GPT-5.6 Sol with production system-level safeguards enabled: **1.5%**
- GPT-5.6 Sol with those system-level guardrails REMOVED (Daybreak Blue access): **2.0%**
- GPT-5.5-Cyber, a model trained to reduce refusals: **57.3%**
- GPT-5.6-Cyber, retrained further for the same: **95.0%**

Stripping the entire system-level screening layer bought 0.5 percentage points. Retraining the model bought 93. For this behaviour class the refusal is overwhelmingly in the weights, and the system-level classifier is a thin outer skin on top of a model that was already going to decline.

Three consequences that change what you build:

- **If a legitimate workflow is being refused, vendor-side 'turn off the filter' access will probably not fix it.** OpenAI says so directly: even without system-level guardrails there remain dual-use prompts where GPT-5.6 Sol 'will refuse to comply', which is the stated reason they trained a separate model. Budget for a differently-trained model or a different vendor, not for a settings change.
- **For threat modelling, the inverse holds.** A leaked system prompt, a jailbroken wrapper, or a self-hosted deployment that drops the vendor's screening layer does not by itself unlock the capability. The guardrail you are trying to defeat, and the one an attacker is trying to defeat, is mostly not the one you can see in the request path.
- **The real control point is model selection.** Choosing between a base and a reduced-refusal variant is a far larger lever than any prompt or classifier you write around either.

**ONE INTERPRETIVE STEP IN THE LABELS, flagged so nobody launders it into certainty.** The source states the figures as '95.0% ... compared with just 1.5% for GPT-5.6 Sol, and 2.0% when used with Daybreak Blue access'. It does not attach the words 'safeguards enabled' to the 1.5% figure; that reading comes from the surrounding text, which says production deploys system-level safeguards and that 'Daybreak Blue access removes those guardrails', and from the page's own prompt-comparison table, which lists 'GPT-5.6-Sol (safeguards enabled)' and 'GPT-5.6 Sol (Daybreak Blue)' as separate rows. The reading is well supported but it IS a reading. What is beyond interpretation, and what the fact actually rests on, is the 0.5-point gap between the two Sol configurations versus the 93-point gap to the retrained model — that comparison holds whichever way the 1.5% row is labelled.

Further boundary conditions, because this is one vendor's internal eval in one capability domain. It measures COMPLETION RATE — willingness to respond — not capability or answer quality; a model can complete 95% of requests and be wrong on most of them. It covers offensive-cyber prompts specifically, where safety training is unusually heavy, so do not assume the same 0.5-point ceiling for domains with lighter training. And the figures are vendor-reported against an unpublished internal dataset with no external replication.
