# Refusal behaviour lives in the weights, not the system layer — across OpenAI's cyber roster as of August 2026, removing system-level guardrails moved completion 1.5% → 2.0% while retraining moved it to 95%

OpenAI published a four-way comparison on an internal eval (Advanced Cybersecurity Completion Rate) measuring how often a model will respond at all to requests involving exploit-chain development, authentication bypass, privilege escalation and similar advanced cyber scenarios. The numbers separate two things that are usually conflated:

- GPT-5.6 Sol with production system-level safeguards enabled: **1.5%**
- GPT-5.6 Sol with those system-level guardrails REMOVED (Daybreak Blue access): **2.0%**
- GPT-5.5-Cyber, a model trained to reduce refusals: **57.3%**
- GPT-5.6-Cyber, retrained further for the same: **95.0%**

Stripping the entire system-level screening layer bought 0.5 percentage points. Retraining the model bought 93. For this behaviour class the refusal is overwhelmingly in the weights, and the system-level classifier is a thin outer skin on top of a model that was already going to decline.

**POPULATION AND DATE, because the title reads like a law otherwise.** These four figures are one vendor's internal eval over a roster fixed as of the August 2026 publication: GPT-5.6 Sol, GPT-5.5-Cyber and GPT-5.6-Cyber. GPT-6 Astra shipped on 2026-09-03, and OpenAI has published no Advanced Cybersecurity Completion Rate figure for it. So the 0.5-versus-93 comparison is a measurement on one model generation, not a constant of language models, and a run citing it should say which generation it describes.

NOTE THE SPREAD BETWEEN THE TWO TRAINED MODELS, because it stops "retraining" being read as a single switch: 57.3% and 95.0% are both reduced-refusal models, one generation apart. How much refusal is removed is itself a training target that moved 37.7 points between releases, and OpenAI attributes the second push to "addressing feedback from security researchers who encountered persistent refusals with the earlier model."

Three consequences that change what you build:

- **If a legitimate workflow is being refused, vendor-side 'turn off the filter' access will probably not fix it.** OpenAI says so directly: even without system-level guardrails there remain dual-use prompts where GPT-5.6 Sol "will refuse to comply", which is the stated reason they trained a separate model. Budget for a differently-trained model or a different vendor, not for a settings change. **One qualification arrived with the next generation, and its direction matters.** Astra's safety overview states that "For users flagged as potentially high risk, we have additionally trained in the ability to adjust the model's refusal boundary to be more conservative and cover a broader range of dual use risks." So the weights-level boundary is now a trained-in parameter the vendor can move at inference time — but the published capability moves it toward MORE refusal, for a risk-flagged subpopulation. Nothing here says a customer can move it the other way, and reading this as "the refusal boundary is now a setting you can loosen" inverts what the source describes.
- **For threat modelling, the inverse holds.** A leaked system prompt, a jailbroken wrapper, or a self-hosted deployment that drops the vendor's screening layer does not by itself unlock the capability. The guardrail you are trying to defeat, and the one an attacker is trying to defeat, is mostly not the one you can see in the request path.
- **Model selection is the real control point — but it is a TRADE, not an upgrade.** See the measured cost below before treating the reduced-refusal variant as strictly better.

*** WILLINGNESS AND CAPABILITY COME APART, AND THE SAME PAGE MEASURES IT. *** Completion rate measures willingness to respond, not capability or answer quality — and the 95%-completing model is WORSE than the base model on two of the four evaluations reported.
- Vulnerability Discovery and Report Writing: "GPT‑5.6‑Cyber performs worse than GPT‑5.6 Sol on this evaluation, which we believe is due to the model sometimes producing shorter, less detailed vulnerability reports."
- ExploitBench (V8 exploitation, harder than ExploitGym): "In the standard setting, which limits agents to 300 turns, GPT‑5.6 Sol (Daybreak Blue) solves tasks more token-efficiently and performs best. If we expand beyond the standard 300-turn setting to 600 turns, the performance gap between the two models narrows."
It does win where it was trained to: GPT-5.6-Cyber outperforms both other models on ExploitGym, and outperformed GPT-5.6 Sol on the internal zero-day severity-and-write-up benchmark.
SO THE DECISION IS TWO-SIDED. Buying a model that will answer costs you quality on the tasks its training did not target — here, the reporting and long-horizon exploitation ends. AND NOTE THE SECOND ORDER EFFECT, which is this pack's reading: the ExploitBench ranking is TURN-BUDGET DEPENDENT, flipping toward parity between 300 and 600 turns, so a comparison between a base and a reduced-refusal model is not stable across harness configuration. That is the same hazard this pack records for benchmark scores generally — a ranking reported without its step limit is not a ranking.

**WHETHER THAT TRADE PERSISTS IS OPEN.** OpenAI claims for Astra "a Pareto improvement in safely completing unsafe requests and avoiding unnecessary refusals to harmless requests" — i.e. that the willingness/safety frontier moved rather than being traded along. That is a vendor claim in a launch post with no figures attached and no cyber-completion measurement, and it speaks to the refusal/over-refusal trade rather than to the willingness-versus-report-quality trade documented above. Treat it as a reason to re-measure on the current generation, not as evidence the trade is gone.

**ONE INTERPRETIVE STEP IN THE LABELS, flagged so nobody launders it into certainty.** The source states the figures as "95.0% ... compared with just 1.5% for GPT-5.6 Sol, and 2.0% when used with Daybreak Blue access". It does not attach the words "safeguards enabled" to the 1.5% figure; that reading comes from the surrounding text, which says production deploys system-level safeguards and that "Daybreak Blue access removes those guardrails", and from the page's own prompt-comparison table, which lists "GPT‑5.6‑Sol (safeguards enabled)" and "GPT‑5.6 Sol (Daybreak Blue)" as separate rows. The reading is well supported but it IS a reading. What is beyond interpretation, and what the fact actually rests on, is the 0.5-point gap between the two Sol configurations versus the 93-point gap to the retrained model — that comparison holds whichever way the 1.5% row is labelled.

Further boundary conditions, because this is one vendor's internal eval in one capability domain. It measures COMPLETION RATE — willingness to respond — not capability or answer quality. It covers offensive-cyber prompts specifically, where safety training is unusually heavy, so do not assume the same 0.5-point ceiling for domains with lighter training. And the figures are vendor-reported against an unpublished internal dataset with no external replication; ExploitGym and ExploitBench were likewise run on OpenAI's "internal implementation". The Preparedness Framework classifications and the CVE-2026-15903 material on the same page are not cited here.
