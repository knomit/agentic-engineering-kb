# A low public incident count can mean a category is well defended, not that it is rare — OWASP tested its 2026 vote against 7,714 real incidents and prompt injection fell out of the top 10 entirely

For the 2026 edition OWASP did something the project had never done: it checked practitioner judgement against a record of what has actually gone wrong. The result is a rare, quantified demonstration that incident-frequency data and risk priority are different things — and it should change how you read any "top risks" list, including this one.

**THE METHOD.** "We pulled together a corpus of 7,714 real incidents from public vulnerability databases and an AI-harm database, and we built classifiers that read them and placed the 6,639 that carried enough detail to sort." The final weighting is explicit: the community vote carries three-quarters, the incident data one quarter. OWASP states the reason for capping the data's influence — "one noisy year of data does not get to overturn the judgment of the people doing the work" — and the reason for including it at all: "A quarter weight is enough to move an entry a tier when the gap between belief and evidence runs wide."

**THE TWO DISAGREEMENTS, and they point in opposite directions.**

*Prompt injection — ranked #1 by practitioners; by raw incident record it "falls out of the top 10 entirely."* OWASP's reading is the important part: "That gap is a defense effect. Teams fight injection hard, so fewer clean exploits reach a public database, and the public count understates the risk that mature teams already spend real money holding off." It stayed at #1.

*Misinformation — voters placed it near the bottom, the incident record near the top.* OWASP calls this "the widest gap in the direction that actually hurts, where the vote sits low and the evidence sits high", and seats it at #7 as a compromise. The mechanism named: "When a model's fluent, confident output drives a decision or a tool call, a wrong answer turns into a wrong action."

Only two entries had belief and evidence agree at the top: Sensitive Information Disclosure at #2, "the one place at the top where belief and evidence simply agree, and where our confidence is highest", and Excessive Agency, where agreement drove the largest move on the list.

**AN INDEPENDENT OPERATOR OBSERVATION ON THE SAME GAP — and be careful what it corroborates.** OpenAI, writing in November 2025, reported that it had not yet seen significant adoption of prompt injection by attackers, while simultaneously treating it as a frontier problem and expecting adversaries to invest heavily in it. That is a second, independent source observing the same low real-world prevalence OWASP's incident corpus found, from a vantage point (a major operator's own telemetry and bug-bounty stream) that does not depend on public vulnerability databases at all. It therefore strengthens the *observation* that the count is genuinely low. It does **not** corroborate OWASP's explanation for it: OpenAI offers no defence-effect account, and its own framing — that attackers have not gotten to it yet, and will — is a different hypothesis about the same number. Both readings argue against deprioritising, for different reasons. Do not cite OpenAI as agreeing that the low count is a defence effect.

**WHAT TO DO WITH THIS.** Do not prioritise your own security work by counting public incidents or CVEs per category. That count is a lagging indicator biased by two things at once: how hard the category is already being defended, and whether failures in it produce the kind of artifact that lands in a vulnerability database at all. A category with few public incidents may be the one the field is spending most to suppress — stop spending and the count is what changes first. Conversely, a failure mode your team dismisses as low-severity, as the field did with misinformation, can be landing more often than anyone's intuition allows, precisely because it produces bad decisions rather than filable vulnerabilities.

**AND ON THE LIST ITSELF.** Knowing the weighting is 75/25 vote-to-evidence tells you what a rank actually encodes: mostly the collective belief of practitioners, adjusted at the margin. That is a defensible construction, and it is not a frequency ranking. Treat the ordering as a prioritisation aid, not a measurement.
