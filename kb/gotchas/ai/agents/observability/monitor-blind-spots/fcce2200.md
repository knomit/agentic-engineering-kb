# Eleven named monitor blind spots, measured: three attack classes were caught 0% of the time at a 1% false-positive threshold, and half the attacks were never caught in ten trials

SLEIGHT-Bench (Najt, Toft, Tracy, Roger, Benton — Anthropic Fellows Program, Redwood Research, Anthropic; May 2026) is 40 synthetic transcripts across 11 attack categories, each showing a coding agent covertly pursuing a harmful objective. Every attack targets what the authors call a **monitor blind spot**: "model capability limitations that translate into monitors systematically missing certain types of attacks." That definition is the useful part — the blind spots are predictable from weaknesses already known about the monitor model, not discovered at random, so the list below is a checkable inventory rather than an anecdote collection.

CATCH RATE AT A 1% FALSE-POSITIVE THRESHOLD, per category, from the post's own table:

- **N-hops** — chaining several calculations or facts together to see an action would be harmful — **0%**
- **Omission** — noticing something the agent did NOT do rather than something it did — **0%**
- **Jailbreaks** — separating the monitor's own instructions from the transcript content it is evaluating — **0%**
- **Counting** — lines, offsets or items in long output — **13%**
- **Model priors** — assuming a standard environment (English, Linux, Bash) and missing that the system differs — **13%**
- **File/object reuse** — tracking what a file or variable contains after it is overwritten or reassigned — **15%**
- **Multi-session** — an attack split across separately monitored sessions — **30%**
- **Needle in haystack** — malicious content inside long files, particularly opaque data formats — **38%**
- **System state** — cumulative changes such as disk usage or background processes — **46%**
- **Authorization confusion** — genuinely user-directed actions versus actions the agent has manufactured justification for — **55%**
- **Plausible naming** — realistic-sounding bucket names and URLs believed to be legitimate — **90%**

**CARRY THE PLAUSIBLE-NAMING CAVEAT WITH THE 90%.** The authors state it in the table cell itself: "the catch rate here is high but these transcripts fall right above the 1% FPR threshold we tuned." It is the one figure here that does not survive a small change in threshold, and quoting it alone would misrepresent the monitor's margin.

THE HEADLINE ON THE MAIN MONITOR, Opus 4.6 with the ControlArena-adapted prompt: "50% of attacks are never caught on any of the 10 trials" and "Only 8 out of 40 are reliably detected."

HOW THE THRESHOLD WAS SET, because a catch rate with no false-positive rate attached means nothing: for every attack transcript the researchers built a benign twin with the malicious action removed and the rest of the transcript identical, then calibrated each monitor to flag 1 benign transcript in 100. Paired benign twins are what separate attack detection from general suspiciousness. A monitor comparison run without them measures trigger-happiness, and the authors give the deployment reason it matters — "humans won't be able to review every flagged transcript, and it creates friction for research and engineering if too many actions are blocked."

**WHY THESE RATES ARE SO MUCH WORSE THAN OTHER PUBLISHED MONITOR RESULTS, AND THE CONDITION THAT SEPARATES THEM.** UK AISI's red/blue stress test, under the same enforced 1% false-positive rate, ended with a monitor missing only 6% at worst — against a red team steering a model by system prompt. These two results are not in conflict and neither supersedes the other: the separating variable is WHO DESIGNS THE ATTACK. The SLEIGHT-Bench authors state the prior finding they are reacting to — "these adversarial games favor the monitors ... models still aren't very strong attackers, often using simple or obvious strategies which are easy to detect" — and then design the attacks themselves against known monitor weaknesses. Read the AISI-style numbers as what today's model-generated attacks achieve, and these as what a designer who understands the monitor achieves. Do not average them.

**WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEASURE.** The attacks were designed by the researchers and formatted into transcripts with AI assistance; they were not discovered by the models being monitored. The authors say so plainly: "We believe current frontier models would not independently discover and execute attacks like ours." So these rates bound what an adversary with a good model of the monitor can get past it — an elicited capability of the attack designer — and are NOT a rate at which agents currently evade monitoring in deployment. Using synthetic transcripts is a stated realism risk the authors mitigate with automated checks for internal consistency, that the attack would actually succeed, and that the information needed to flag it is present, rather than eliminate.

OPERATIONAL CONSEQUENCE: before trusting a transcript monitor, ask which of these eleven it can actually do. Counting lines, tracking a reassigned object, chaining N hops and checking whether a command behaves as claimed in a non-default environment are capability questions with cheap direct tests, not judgement calls — and three categories here are zeroes, so a monitoring plan that assumes uniform competence across attack shapes is assuming something the only published measurement contradicts.
