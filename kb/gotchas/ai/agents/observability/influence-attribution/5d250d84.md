# Agent influence on a human decision leaves no audit trail in either place you would look for it: 77% of analysts reused their agent's argument, only 15% credited it anywhere, and 52% rated the recommendation as more their own than the agent's

There are exactly two cheap ways to audit whether an agent shaped a human's decision: read the human's written rationale, or ask the human. Anthropic's randomized trial of 50 analysts (published 2026-10-27) measured both, and neither works.

THE RATIONALE. Asked "Briefly, why that choice rather than the alternative?", 77% of analysts repeated their agent's argument in at least one answer, sometimes word for word, and only 15% credited the agent anywhere. So the written justification carries the agent's reasoning at a high rate while naming it at a low one. A reviewer reading the rationale sees a human argument.

THE SELF-REPORT. On the post-task survey, 52% of analysts rated the final recommendation as more their own than their agent's, and 35% as mostly or entirely their own. Self-rated ownership and the measured shift toward the agent's coin-flipped lean moved by comparable amounts (about 65 points against about 79), so people who felt the decision was theirs were shifted about as much as people who did not. Confidence did not separate the two groups either: analysts who followed the lean on all four decisions rated their confidence 71 out of 100, against 73 for everyone else. 6 of 48 correctly guessed what the study was about.

THE OPERATIONAL CONSEQUENCE. On an open judgment call there is usually no later ground truth that exposes the influence — the work looks sound whichever option was chosen — so provenance has to be captured at the time, in the system, rather than reconstructed afterwards. If you need to know what the agent contributed to a decision, log the agent's side of the exchange as a first-class artifact alongside the submitted answer. Do not build a control, an attestation or a disclosure requirement on the author's recollection or on a "was this AI-assisted?" checkbox: both were measured here and both under-report.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT SAY. It is not a finding that the analysts were concealing anything. The paper's reading is that they may not notice the influence, which is why asking them afterwards does not help. Nor does it say the reused arguments were wrong — reuse was measured, correctness of the reused argument was not. The 25-person breakdown behind the 52% appears only in a figure's description and not in the prose, and the ownership question was asked once, at the end, of 48 respondents.
