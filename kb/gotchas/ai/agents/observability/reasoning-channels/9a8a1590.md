# Forbidding a model to reason in one channel moved the reasoning into another observable channel rather than removing it — and controllability is confounded by reasoning length, so it must be reported per length band

Instructed not to reason about a question in its analysis channel and to think about something else instead, four models of one vendor's line behaved four different ways on the same prompt. The oldest continued reasoning about the question anyway. The next explicitly recognised its own violation mid-reasoning and stopped. One complied by generating unrelated imagery. The newest generated NO analysis-channel text at all — and made question-related tool calls in the commentary channel instead.

That last case is the trap. Measured on the constrained channel alone it is perfect compliance; measured on what the system actually did, the reasoning simply relocated to a channel the instruction did not name. A constraint on one reasoning surface is not a reduction in reasoning, and an evaluation that scores only the named surface will record a success.

The same source reports a second, independent measurement caveat: chain-of-thought controllability is heavily confounded by chain-of-thought length, because longer reasoning is harder to control all else equal, so scores must be reported as a function of length rather than as a single number. Within the 750-1,250 token band the vendor reports successful compliance with a requested CoT instruction in 1.7%, 16.1%, 23.2%, 44.8% and 60.9% of cases for five models of ascending generation — figures that are only comparable because they are cut to one length band. Controllability also did not improve uniformly: one model's gain held across all three datasets but not across all eight instruction types, and it could satisfy a purely formatting constraint (alternating letter case) while failing to avoid reasoning about the original question.

Two operational consequences. When you constrain or suppress a reasoning channel, enumerate every other channel the system can write to — tool calls, commentary, scratch files, message content — and monitor those too, or you have moved the evidence rather than removed the behaviour. And when you compare controllability or any other per-token property across models or prompts, control for output length first; an uncontrolled comparison mostly measures verbosity.

What this does NOT mean: the newest model was not shown to be concealing anything. All four models answered the underlying question correctly, and relocating reasoning to a channel the instruction did not cover is as consistent with literal compliance as with evasion. The finding is about what the measurement can and cannot see, not about intent.
