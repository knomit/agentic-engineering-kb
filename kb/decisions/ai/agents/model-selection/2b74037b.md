# Pick the smallest model that passes your evals; few-shot on a small model often beats zero-shot on a large one

Concrete comparisons practitioners report: Claude Haiku with 10-shot examples outperforming zero-shot Opus on their task, and DistilBERT at 67M parameters beating many LLMs at classification while costing about 5% of the latency and price. Flow engineering — several structured calls to a smaller model — frequently beats one call to the largest available.

The decision rule is to start from the smallest model that clears your eval bar and escalate only on measured failure, rather than defaulting to the frontier model and never revisiting it. Model capability is the least durable part of your system anyway; the durable parts are evals, guardrails, caching, and the data flywheel.

Two caveats that keep this from being blanket advice. First, it is downstream of having evals — without them "smallest that works" is unmeasurable and you will ship a silent quality regression. Second, it cuts the other way on hard reasoning and agentic tool use, where Anthropic found upgrading the model beat doubling the token budget on an older one. So: smallest model for well-specified, high-volume, narrow steps; best model for the open-ended planning step.

STALENESS: source verified 2026-07-26 as still live and still stating all of the above; the page carries a 2024-06-08 publication date and no update notice. The "Haiku beats zero-shot Opus" exemplar refers to the Claude 3 generation and the GPT-4 era — several generations old. Treat the exemplar as an illustration of the shape, not as a current benchmark, and re-measure the small-vs-large gap on your own task: the capability spread between tiers has moved repeatedly since, in both directions, and nothing here licenses a claim about today's Haiku versus today's Opus.
