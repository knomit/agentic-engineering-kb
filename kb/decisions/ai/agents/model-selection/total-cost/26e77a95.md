# Per-token price does not predict agent cost — the cheaper-listed model cost nearly double over 417 tasks because cache-read pricing dominated

IBM Research measured a CodeAct agent on the AppWorld Test Challenge (417 tasks) and found Claude Sonnet cost $79 total ($0.19/task) against GPT-4.1's $155 ($0.37/task) — nearly double — despite GPT-4.1 having the lower per-token list price. Their attribution: cache-read pricing. Sonnet's cheaper cache reads more than compensated for a higher base rate and for longer execution trajectories.

This is the specific reason a routing or model-selection decision made from a pricing page is unreliable for agents. An agent replays a growing prefix on every turn, so the dominant cost term is cache reads on that prefix, not fresh input or output tokens. Two models with the same headline input price and different cache-read discounts have materially different agent costs, and trajectory length — how many turns the model takes to finish — multiplies the difference in whichever direction the model is worse.

The corollary for routing design: IBM argue routing is a systems optimisation problem, not a task-difficulty classification problem, because the router must weigh cost, quality, latency, compliance and reliability at once, and because end-to-end latency is dominated by infrastructure state — which hardware the model runs on, whether the cache is warm, how busy the endpoint is — rather than by the model's theoretical speed. They report a router optimising jointly rather than by difficulty reaching 84% accuracy at $93 and 83 seconds, which they describe as a 21% cost reduction and 9% latency reduction against running Opus alone for a 4% accuracy drop, at a router overhead of roughly 6 ms and 2 kB per task.

What decides it: route on measured end-to-end cost per completed task on your own workload, not on price-per-token and not on estimated task difficulty. The 6 ms overhead figure means the router itself is never the reason not to route; the reason not to route is that a difficulty-based router explores the wrong tradeoff curve and can cost more than the single strong model it was meant to replace. Note the accuracy figures come from the authors of the router being evaluated — treat the 21%/9% as vendor-reported, while the $79-vs-$155 comparison is the more portable finding.
