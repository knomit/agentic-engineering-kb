# In a manual release process the elapsed time is queueing, not work: 16 days from check-in to production, under an hour of build and deploy, almost 14 days waiting for a person to start a step

When Amazon measured its own release pipeline before automating it, the decomposition was: "We discovered that it was taking, on average, 16 days from code check-in to production", of which "We spent less than an hour to build and deploy the new code" and "The remainder of the time, almost 14 days, we spent waiting for team members to start a build." The headline pilot team then "achieved a 90 percent reduction in the overall time."

The operational consequence is a measurement instruction, not a platitude: instrument the WAIT BETWEEN steps, not the duration of steps. Making a step faster buys you the step; removing the human trigger in front of it buys you days. A pipeline whose stage timings all look healthy can still have almost all of its elapsed time outside any stage, where nothing is being measured at all.

For agent pipelines this separates two things that get conflated under "human in the loop". A human APPROVAL gate is a control with a stated purpose — and the deployment-safety material in this pack argues for keeping specific ones. A human TRIGGER is someone being the reason a step starts, with no decision attached. The 14 days were triggers. Auditing which of your gates are decisions and which are merely triggers is the cheapest latency work available, and it does not weaken any control.

Scope and caveats: these are Amazon's figures for its own internal transition, reported by Amazon, with no date given in the article text and no independent measurement. The 90 percent is for the "headline pilot team", not an organization-wide average — compressing it to "automation cut release time 90 percent" drops both the pilot framing and the fact that the saving came almost entirely from one component, the wait.
