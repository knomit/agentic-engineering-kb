# Per-tenant isolation failed for organisational reasons, not technical ones: dedicated containers removed the workload mixing that had been absorbing launch spikes, so every product team inherited capacity planning and forgot to do it

An operator serving over a billion users moved its storage platform from one shared multi-tenant container to dedicated per-use-case containers and then back to shared by default. Both directions are documented with the reason each failed, which makes this the rare isolation decision with evidence on both sides.

WHY SHARED FAILED FIRST. Three mechanisms, all of them the enforced-unit problem: logical partition keys chosen by clients collided on obvious names — "we have frequent collisions on commonly used partition keys, such as user_id or workspace_id, across different product use cases" — so unrelated products shared one key's throughput budget; noisy neighbours on a shared physical partition let "workloads operating on data for niche product features" disrupt "core login and signup flows"; and storage growth alone shrank throughput. Dedicated containers fixed all three.

WHY DEDICATED FAILED NEXT, AND THIS IS THE PART THAT TRANSFERS. Isolation destroyed workload mixing, and workload mixing had been silently absorbing launch spikes. In the shared container "any particular new use case going from 0 QPS to 1 million QPS was unlikely to significantly shift the total aggregate usage"; on an isolated one the same launch "might represent 1,000x more load than the baseline attributed to only internal dogfooding". The published arithmetic: a workload tested at 1K RU/s and launched at 1M RU/s consumes 0.005% then 5% of a shared container's headroom (100M RU/s provisioned, 80% utilised, so 20M spare), versus 10% then 10,000% of a new dedicated container provisioned at 10K RU/s. So the dedicated tenant's own load test tells it nothing about launch.

The consequence was organisational: "teams now needed to remember to pre-provision containers to the expected level of throughput for their launches. Product teams would often ship their features to production and forget to pre-scale and we'd sit in SEV rooms on launch day". Monitoring a dedicated container "required intimate product knowledge of when traffic would shift or grow", which "failed to scale organizationally with a wide variance in how much diligence each service's owning team paid". The verdict: "No lone, central team doing planning resulted in many teams planning poorly."

WHAT DECIDED IT IN THE END was not choosing a side but making shared tenancy safe enough to return to, with three specific controls: throttling the hottest keys first "prevents noisy neighbors from consuming all of the available throughput for a given physical partition"; request-tiering QoS so "offline workloads, such as bulk inserts or data extracts" are "throttled first, preventing throttling on higher priority, interactive requests"; and a vendor-built serverless mode letting "every physical partition to scale up to 10K RU/s" with "RU-usage-based splits in addition to storage-usage-based splits", which removed manual scaling. Reported outcome: "We have not had any provisioning or noisy neighbor incidents in the months since, despite accelerating product development."

THE CONDITION THAT DECIDES THIS, stated as a rule: per-tenant isolation is right when each tenant's team can and will do its own capacity planning. When they cannot, isolation converts a central team's tractable statistical-multiplexing problem into N teams' forecasting problems, and the aggregate gets worse. Buy back isolation inside the shared pool — hot-key throttling plus priority tiers — rather than by partitioning the pool.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: the dedicated-container phase was not a mistake that achieved nothing. It "saved us from many site-wide outages, limiting the blast radius to feature or service degradation outages" while the shared-tenancy controls did not yet exist. Scope: this is one operator's account of one managed document database, with no counterfactual measurement of the two designs run side by side.
