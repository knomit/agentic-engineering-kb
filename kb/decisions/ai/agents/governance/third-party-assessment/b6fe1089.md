# A deep-access embedded red-team buys monitor-placement findings a black-box engagement cannot reach and pays in publication control — and in this engagement the evaluators had no right to describe the redactions, the redaction-summary mechanism they themselves recommend elsewhere

Two public accounts exist of the same engagement: a three-week pilot, "from July 21st to August 11th", in which "a METR employee and a Redwood Research employee conducted a pilot high-access exercise" against OpenAI's internal misalignment monitoring system. Read as a pair, they price this assessment modality.

**What the access bought, and it is a class of finding nothing else reaches.** "This exercise involved a deep level of access." The evaluators had access to OpenAI's internal codebase, to the misalignment monitors' prompts and configuration, and to internal documentation, and they "conducted technical interviews with approximately a dozen researchers and engineers". The access was physically bounded: "they worked on OpenAI-issued devices, which never left the premises, and were accompanied while onsite." The yield was architectural rather than behavioural — where the monitoring sits relative to the agent, which black-box probing cannot see. Among the findings: client-controllable headers that disable monitoring for new agents, a compaction-boundary trigger an agent can postpone indefinitely, and "two unmonitored internal agentic tools that were thought to have read-only permissions" — a read-only assumption used to justify not monitoring, and never validated.

**What it cost: the publication is not the assessment, and the integrity signal was not available.** "we agreed upfront with OpenAI that we would only publish a relatively brief and high-level public post", and "we are only publishing a small subset of the content of the private report". The full output was "a 61-page internal report"; "A 38-page version redacted by OpenAI was shared with the leadership teams at METR and Redwood." OpenAI "had the right to redact from this public version" whatever it considered confidential, and the evaluators state they "did not have a unilateral right to describe such redactions".

That last clause is the sharp one, because it is the same evaluator organisation whose own published template for third-party work asks for a redaction summary "describing how the redaction process impacted the set of conclusions it could publicly substantiate", and which did carry a redaction summary statement in its Hugging Face incident report. So the mechanism that makes a redacted report legible is applied by this evaluator on some engagements and contractually unavailable on others. **A reader cannot assume it is present: check for it per report rather than per evaluator.** Where it is absent, the published scope is not merely partial, it is partial by an unstated amount. The evaluators' own framing is that this exercise does not provide "robust public transparency".

**What it cost: remediation status is the assessed party's claim, and the two sides' quantifiers already differ.** The evaluators report that "OpenAI has stated that all of the vulnerabilities we identified" that impact production "have been patched", and add: "We have not evaluated these claims." OpenAI's own account of the same exercise states something narrower — "This red-teaming exercise identified several vulnerabilities that impacted our monitoring stack at that time. Most of these have now been patched; one remaining fix is still in progress." The two are reconcilable on scope, production-impacting versus all, but the slogans a hurried reader compresses them to are not: *all, patched* against *most, one outstanding*. Neither is independently verified by anyone.

**What it cost: the finding is dated and the system moved under it.** The assessment covers "the internal misalignment monitoring system as it existed on and before July 21st", excluding "changes that were in progress at the time of the exercise", and OpenAI "was expanding their monitoring system during our exercise". OpenAI confirms the ordering: "This pilot was conducted before we expanded our misalignment monitoring across model training, evaluation, internal use, and external deployment." The coverage of the current system is not something this exercise established, and the evaluators say so.

**The decision, and what decides it.** If the question is whether a monitor is positioned where the agent cannot get upstream of it, deep access is the modality that answers and the price is control over neither published scope nor fix verification. If the question needs an auditable public claim about the present state of a control, this modality does not produce one. The limit is on completeness and on remediation verification, not on accuracy: the evaluators state "we endorse each of the findings that are presented in this write-up".

**Two things to carry rather than infer.** Deep access did not make everything estimable — the evaluators name their own blocked questions, "we were unable to estimate the volume of such traffic due to access and time constraints" and "we did not have the ability to check whether agents had, in practice, exploited this vulnerability" — and they attach their own uncertainty to the coverage figures they do give ("although we have substantial uncertainty in both directions"). And the arrangement is meant to recur rather than being a one-off: OpenAI says "we see value in continuing to give independent researchers the opportunity to test it through embedded assessments".
