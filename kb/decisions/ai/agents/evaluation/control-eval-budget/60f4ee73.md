# Spend a control-eval budget on more environment × side-task pairs, not on a better simulator: the spread between side-task categories is a 20.2-point SD while the simulator's own bias is 4.6 points, and four extra grounding affordances bought nothing

Once you are running simulated control evaluations ([[1f271055]] has the method), the budget question is where the next unit of engineering goes: into making the simulator more faithful, or into running the thing over more of your deployment. Redwood's numbers answer it, and the answer is not the intuitive one.

**THE RATIO THAT DECIDES IT.** "side-task categories have a safety SD of 20.2 percentage points, while simulation's RMS bias" — the instrument's own error — "is only 4.6 points." The quantity you are trying to measure varies four times more across *what the attack is trying to do* than your measuring device distorts it. So an engineer who improves the simulator is polishing a 4.6-point error term while a 20.2-point spread sits unsampled. Coverage first.

**AND THE AFFORDANCES DO NOT PAY.** Redwood tried giving the simulator more to work with — access to the attack policy's reasoning, an executable sandbox, a trajectory database, and forking — and reports "Many grounding affordances are unnecessary." Nor does telling the truth cost anything: "Informing monitors doesn't seem to reduce SCE accuracy", so you do not need to hide from your own monitors that some of their inputs are simulated, and a pipeline built around concealing it is paying for nothing. Note the hedge in the original on that second one — *doesn't seem to* — and that both are reported on their own setup rather than proven in general.

**READ THE TAILS, NOT THE MEAN.** "Our results suggest that there is a high degree of variance" across environments and tasks. With a 20.2-point SD between side-task categories, a single pooled safety number is close to meaningless as a deployment claim: an average that looks acceptable can hide a category where the monitor catches nothing. Report per-category and per-environment results and act on the worst, which is also what makes the coverage spend above worth making.

**THE CONDITION THIS HOLDS UNDER, AND IT IS NOT "GROUNDING IS OVERRATED".** The SCE simulator already starts from a real transcript in a real repository, which is the strongest grounding available; the finding is that marginal affordances on top of that saturate. [[9693b43b]] measures the opposite end of the same curve and finds grounding paying heavily — grounding an auditor in real deployment artifacts raised audit realism from 4.6% to 32.8% on reward-hacking scenarios, and did almost nothing on a shutdown-resistance scenario where the scenario itself was implausible. Put together: grounding buys a great deal while the environment is synthetic and the scenario plausible, and stops buying once the environment is genuinely real. Which half of that curve you are on decides whether this fact's advice applies to you.

**A READING TRAP: 4.6 APPEARS THREE TIMES IN THIS MATERIAL AND MEANS THREE DIFFERENT THINGS.** Simulation's RMS bias is 4.6 *percentage points*; SCEs beat the full BashArena safety prediction with "only 4.6% as many task-pair draws as deployment"; and [[9693b43b]]'s ungrounded audit-realism baseline is 4.6%. Do not let a summary collapse them.
