# Agent capability is a curve against token budget, not a score — and human task duration predicts the budget with a fitted exponent of 0.7–1.0

UK AISI measured agent performance against test-time compute budget across 11 frontier models (April 2025–April 2026) on cyber tasks and 20 (March 2023–December 2025) on software engineering, and published on 2026-07-02. The finding an engineer can act on is a sizing rule and a stopping rule.

THE SIZING RULE. Required tokens scale with how long the task takes a human, and "the relationship has a fitted exponent of roughly 0.7–1.0", fitted "across 211 software-engineering tasks (METR, blue) and 78 cyber capture-the-flag tasks (AISI, red)". In orders of magnitude: a one-minute task costs thousands of tokens, an hour-long task millions, week-long work approaches billions. So estimate an agent task's budget from the human duration of the work, not from the prompt size — and expect the tail to be long: "Around 8% of tasks were only solved once the budget reached 10M+ tokens", some needing up to 50M, and on Epoch's MirrorCode one model used up to a billion.

THE STOPPING RULE, and it is the more useful half. "More compute helps most where an agent can check its own work e.g. running code, testing an exploit, and little where feedback is weak or absent." HealthBench is the published counter-example: it plateaus, and extra budget buys almost nothing. This is a concrete predictor of when to spend and when to stop — if the loop has no executable verifier, raising the budget is not the lever, and the money goes to building the verifier instead. Compare: TerminalBench performance kept improving at 10x the public evaluation budget; METR's tasks rose about 25% from a 1M to a 10M budget; Humanity's Last Exam gained roughly 22% up to 5M.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR READING ANYONE ELSE'S NUMBERS. A benchmark score is a point on a curve at whatever budget the harness allowed, and AISI's own trend estimates move sharply with it: "the fitted frontier trend is ~60% steeper when horizons are estimated at 50M tokens rather than 2.5M tokens", and "one recent frontier model's horizon rose from around 40 minutes at a 2.5M-token budget to around 4 hours at a 50M-token budget". A six-fold difference in claimed time horizon for the same model, from budget alone. Restricted-budget scores, AISI warns, "can make model comparisons unequal, lead decision-makers to underestimate agent capabilities, and obscure the true scale of risks". Two models compared at different budgets are not comparable, and a low score may mean an under-resourced run rather than a weak model.

AISI'S RECOMMENDED PRACTICE, which transfers to a private eval suite: test across multiple budgets including very large ones on the hardest tasks; report reach and reliability AGAINST budget rather than a single number; define a "minimum informative budget" by confirming reach has plateaued; and work on forecasting high-budget performance from cheaper runs.

THE NON-MONOTONICITY, which is easy to miss and matters for model upgrades: newer models generally convert extra compute into larger gains than their predecessors, but not everywhere — on a minority of tasks, "roughly 10–30% depending on the suite", newer models do worse than the models they replace. A model swap is not a uniform improvement, so a regression suite over your own tasks is not optional.

LIMITS THE SOURCE STATES: there is significant variation around the duration-to-compute trend, and the relationship may differ by domain and may shift as models get more token-efficient. Three questions are left open — where compute reliably buys capability and why, whether high-budget results can be estimated from cheap runs, and how broadly human task time predicts compute across domains.
