# One alarm that rolls up every service and canary stops ALL of a team's in-flight deployments in that Region — coupling unrelated changes is the design, not a bug in it

In Amazon's described pipeline each microservice carries its own high-severity alarm on fault rate, latency, CPU, memory, disk, errors in logs and failing health checks. Above those sits a **team-level high-severity aggregate alarm** that rolls up every microservice alarm plus the canary alarms, and the rule attached to it is deliberately broad: if the aggregate enters ALARM, "all of the team's ongoing deployments" in that Region roll back. Starting a NEW production deployment is also blocked while it is in ALARM, and organization-wide alarms can block too; a developer can override the block, and the stated purpose of the override is recovering from a high-severity issue rather than getting on with routine work.

The tradeoff being made, and it is the whole point of the design. A change to service A can be rolled back because service B is unhealthy, and the two may have nothing to do with each other. That is accepted because during an incident the cheapest hypothesis is "something we are deploying right now", and the cost of unwinding an unrelated good change is far below the cost of hand-diagnosing which of six concurrent rollouts is responsible while the Region is degraded. The system also auto-rolls back on anomalies in standard metrics with no alarm defined for them — request count dropping to zero, latency or faults well above normal — so absence of signal counts as signal.

WHEN THIS STOPS BEING THE RIGHT SHAPE. The coupling is affordable only when rollback is cheap and safe, which is a property you have to establish separately: a change that cannot be rolled back (a protocol or format change — see [[3cd8de1c]]) turns an aggregate-alarm trip from an inconvenience into an outage of its own. For agent systems the analogous unit is the fleet-wide config, prompt or model-version rollout, where the blast radius of a bad change is the same shape but the health signal is worse: a degraded agent usually keeps returning 200s, so the aggregate alarm has to be fed task-outcome metrics rather than transport-level ones or it will never fire.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT LICENSE. It is not an argument for one alarm instead of per-service alarms. The per-service alarms still exist and still scope their own rollbacks; the aggregate is an additional, coarser trigger layered on top, and a team that replaces its specific alarms with one rolled-up alarm loses the ability to tell which service is unhealthy.

SCOPE. One organisation's internal practice as described in 2020; the structure is stated plainly, the rate of unnecessary rollbacks it causes is not measured anywhere in the source.
