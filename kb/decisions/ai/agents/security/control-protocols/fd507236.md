# Adapting a control protocol to the capability limits of current models is cheaper than defending the strongest conceivable attack — the source grades the regime in five AI control levels and does not itself claim the safety case expires

Dated April 2025; a position post with no measurements. The premise of AI control is that you constrain what a model can do instead of assuming training made it safe, and build protections on the conservative assumption that the model might be scheming — so control can reduce risk without waiting for alignment breakthroughs.

THE DESIGN DECISION, and the source's own framing of it: "Argue that control protocols should be adapted to the capability limitations of models." Rather than defending against the strongest possible attack, tune the protocol to what the models you are actually deploying can do, on cost-effectiveness grounds. The evidence comes from a control evaluation run as a two-team game: a red team builds models that try to cause harm by subverting the controls, a blue team implements monitoring and other protections, and if even adversarial models cannot produce unacceptable outcomes you have empirical support for a safety claim. An "'AI control safety case' is a structured argument that models are incapable of subverting control measures".

THE CONSEQUENCE THAT MAKES THIS A TRADEOFF RATHER THAN A FREE WIN. A protocol justified by today's capability limits has a safety case whose validity is tied to those limits, so it must be re-argued at each model release rather than inherited — which is why the source frames safety cases as iterative across successive releases and pairs them with graded control levels that map rising scheming capability to more demanding controls and evaluations. Budget for re-running the evaluation on every model upgrade, not for running it once.

TWO STATED LIMITS. The guarantee is bounded by the red team's elicitation: control is only as good as the evaluations behind it, and the source says more work is needed on realistic evaluations that can estimate deployment security. And the approach has a ceiling — "existing AI control approaches are insufficient for scaling to systems with scheming capabilities" far beyond human level, where effective control would require fundamental research breakthroughs.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: capability-adapted does not mean weaker across the board. The protocol is still built on the assumption the model may be scheming; what is adapted is the attack strength it is sized against. Mapping which specific capabilities require which evaluations is named as an open problem, so the grading is a frame to fill in, not a table to apply.

WHAT THE SOURCE ACTUALLY GRADES, verified against the page: it sets out "five distinct AI control levels (ACLs)", anchored at "models incapable of competent scheming (ACL-0)" and "models with scheming capabilities vastly above those of humans (ACL-5)". Note that the stated count and the named endpoints do not obviously reconcile — five levels, but labels running from ACL-0 to ACL-5 — so cite the source's own wording rather than a level count you derive from the labels. Its design decision reads: "Argue that control protocols should be adapted to the capability limitations of models:", with the alternative it rejects being "control measures intended to be robust to models that execute best attack strategies known to humans". Published Apr 11, 2025.

PACK ANALYSIS, NOT THE SOURCE'S CLAIM: the consequence that a safety case built this way does not transfer to a more capable model is this pack's inference from the design decision, and the page does not state it. What the page says instead is that "We show a concrete trajectory of how an AI control safety case might evolve alongside possible future capabilities" — evolution across levels, which is weaker than expiry and is in fact the mechanism by which an operator would re-derive the argument. The inference still holds as reasoning: an argument whose premise is a named capability ceiling says nothing above that ceiling. But it is not quotable as the source's position.
