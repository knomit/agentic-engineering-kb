# A robustness score measured against your own trained adversary bounds nothing — hold out the attacker, not just the environments

Two credible operators published within a week of each other on automated red-teaming, and they set opposite defaults for how to read the resulting number. Keep both; the conditions below decide which applies.

OPENAI'S POSITION (2026-07-15), stated through its reporting: after adversarially training GPT-5.6 on attacks from its GPT-Red attacker model, "GPT-5.6 Sol fails on only 0.05% of GPT-Red's direct prompt injections", with attack success rate computed over held-out ENVIRONMENTS. The scaling story is treated as evidence the approach works: successive production models since GPT-5.3 were trained against progressively stronger red-teamers and GPT-Red's success rate against them dropped monotonically.

UK AISI'S POSITION (2026-07-23), from red-teaming other labs' control monitors: "If agents can outperform our red team, a monitor that stops our red team might still be beaten by a misaligned agent." AISI additionally reports that red-teaming locates weaknesses but does not yield a safety estimate — "Finding monitor weaknesses is one thing; estimating the overall efficacy of a safety measure from those findings is another" — and that it has no method for producing overall assessments.

WHAT SEPARATES THEM, and it is not a disagreement about facts: OpenAI's number holds the ENVIRONMENTS out and leaves the ATTACKER in the defender's training distribution. AISI's caution is about the ATTACKER axis specifically. So ask three questions of any published robustness rate before acting on it:
1. WAS THE ATTACKER HELD OUT? If the defence was trained on this attacker's output, the score measures how well the training converged, not how a novel adversary fares. A near-zero rate against an in-distribution attacker is consistent with a much higher rate against a different one, and nothing in the number distinguishes the two.
2. IS THE RED TEAM AN UPPER BOUND OR A LOWER BOUND ON THE REAL ADVERSARY? Both organisations assume the latter and say so. AISI states the assumption explicitly and notes its own automation iterates against monitor feedback in ways a deployed agent could not, which cuts the other way and makes its attacker unrealistically strong on one axis and unrealistically weak on another.
3. IS THERE A SEPARATE GENERALISATION TEST, AND OF WHAT? OpenAI does publish one — 84% success on a novel scenario suite versus 13% for humans — but that test targets GPT-5.1 and establishes the ATTACKER generalising to new scenarios, not the hardened DEFENDER holding up against a new attacker. A generalisation claim has a subject; check which side of the game it is about.

4. WHAT IS THE ATTEMPT BUDGET, AND IS THE RATE PER QUERY OR PER SCENARIO? This is the axis that moves a number by an order of magnitude, and both shapes appear in the SAME document. OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra system card reports internal GPT-Red indirect prompt-injection robustness as defender success averaged PER DEFENDER QUERY, "where robustness climbed from 96.23% to 99.79%", with instruction-hierarchy robustness "saturated ... at 99.99%". The same card reports an external evaluation by Gray Swan on 1,810 curated attacks from its IPI Arena, scored as P(at least one success within n attempts): "The estimated attack success rate across 15 attempts per scenario was 8.5%, compared with 27.0% for GPT-5.6 Sol." A 0.21% per-query failure rate and an 8.5% per-scenario-over-15-attempts success rate are not in conflict — they are different quantities with different denominators — but a reader who compresses the first into "99.79% robust to prompt injection" is wrong by more than an order of magnitude about an adversary who gets fifteen tries, and a real attacker always gets more than one. The card's multiturn jailbreak evaluation states the correct form explicitly: "we report the worst-case defender success rate as a function of attacker budget: for a budget of n attempts, the model succeeds only if it avoids unsafe assistance across all n attacks." Demand n. A robustness figure without an attempt budget is a per-query figure, and a per-query figure is the most favourable framing available.

NOTE WHAT THE NEWER CARD DOES ANSWER, ON QUESTION 1. Its static jailbreak evaluations now state that "half the prompts in each evaluation were generated by a held-out attacker, one that was not used to generate training data", which is the attacker-axis holdout AISI's caution asks for, on half the set. It also reports that the same evaluations give lower refusal rates for the older models the attack prompts were built against, and says so — those prompts "were intentionally adversarial and designed to jailbreak earlier models", so the comparison flatters the newest model by construction. Both of these are the vendor disclosing a limit in its own favour-direction, which is the behaviour to look for in a robustness report; neither removes question 1 for the internal GPT-Red numbers above, which remain in-distribution for the attacker.

Provenance note on the Gray Swan figure: it is a third party's evaluation reported BY OpenAI in OpenAI's own card, not an independent publication, so it illustrates and bounds the divergence rather than corroborating it from outside.

PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE for someone shipping an agent: adversarial training against a strong automated attacker is the best-evidenced way to move the number, and the number it moves is not the one you want. Budget for an evaluation by an attacker your training never saw, and treat every self-reported robustness rate as conditional on the adversary that produced it AND on how many attempts that adversary was given.
