# The Illusion of Thinking is contested: separate reasoning capability from output-length limits

Shojaee et al. (Apple, 2025) tested reasoning models on controllable puzzle environments (Tower of Hanoi and similar) and reported three regimes — non-reasoning models win at low complexity, reasoning models win at medium, both collapse to zero accuracy beyond a threshold — plus a striking claim that reasoning effort RISES then FALLS as problems get harder, with token budget still unspent.

The paper drew a direct published rebuttal (A. Lawsen, arXiv:2506.09250) alleging the collapse is an artefact of experimental design, on three grounds: (1) at the reported failure threshold, Tower of Hanoi requires more moves than the model's output token limit permits — 15 disks is >32,000 moves — and models stated they were truncating; (2) the grading script scored truncated-but-strategically-correct output as a reasoning failure, conflating reasoning failures with practical output limits; (3) some River Crossing instances are, in the rebuttal's words, "mathematically impossible instances for N > 5 due to insufficient boat capacity", and models were penalised for not solving them. Asking for a generating function instead of an enumerated move list produced high accuracy on instances originally scored as total failures.

Do not treat either as settled. What is safe to act on regardless of who is right: never conclude an agent "cannot do" a task from a benchmark whose correct answer is exponentially long, because you are measuring the output channel, not the reasoning. Two practical rules follow — have the agent emit a program or generating rule rather than an enumerated result whenever the result is long, and make your eval harness distinguish truncation, refusal, and genuine error, since scoring all three as wrong is exactly the flaw alleged here.

GENERALISATION WORTH KEEPING: this dispute is the cleanest published example of a benchmark measuring its own instrumentation rather than the system under test. The three alleged defects — an output channel too small for the correct answer, a grader that cannot tell truncation from error, and unsolvable items scored as failures — are all reproducible in an ordinary in-house agent eval, and none of them shows up as anything other than a low score.
