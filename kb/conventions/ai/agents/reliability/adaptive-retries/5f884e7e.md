# Adaptive retries: price a timeout higher than a 500, and refill the retry budget at 1 token per 10-100 successes

The standard retry recipe — up to N attempts with exponential backoff and jitter — adds load to a dependency at precisely the moment it is failing. Amazon's replacement, 'adaptive retries', keeps backoff-with-jitter and adds two token buckets. It is shipped in the AWS SDK and CLI, so this is a description of production behaviour rather than a proposal.

MECHANISM 1 — RETRY QUOTA. A token bucket that every retry must pay from. Two details carry the value:
- DIFFERENT ERRORS COST DIFFERENT AMOUNTS. A retry after a TIMEOUT costs more tokens than a retry after an internal server error, because a timeout is evidence the dependency is overloaded enough not to answer at all, while a 500 may be an isolated fault. Most hand-rolled retry layers treat all failures identically and so retry hardest against the failure that least tolerates it.
- REFILL IS FRACTIONAL. A success returns a fraction of a token — around 0.1 or 0.01, i.e. you earn one retry per 10 or per 100 successes, up to the bucket cap. Errors refill nothing. The behaviour this produces is the goal: retries are freely available while the error rate is low, and self-throttle as it rises.

MECHANISM 2 — CLIENT-SIDE RATE LIMITING. A second token bucket, applied to ORIGINAL requests as well as retries, whose fill rate and capacity increase after a success and are reduced on a throttling response. This adapts the client to the dependency's current capacity rather than its nominal one.

ORDERING: apply client-side rate limiting FIRST, then check the retry quota. And place both as close to the source of the request as possible — amplification compounds per hop, so a limiter deep in the chain has less to work with.

WHY IT MATTERS AT ALL, in one formula: with a call chain of depth n where each node retries k times, the deepest dependency receives k^n requests. Three attempts across five hops is 243x. Agents make this worse than ordinary services because a tool call often fans out through several services and the agent itself may retry the whole tool call on top of whatever the SDK already did — stacking a retry loop in the agent on a retrying client is how you build the exponent without noticing.
