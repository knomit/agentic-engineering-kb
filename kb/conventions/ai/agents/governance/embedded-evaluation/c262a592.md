# Embedded evaluation puts a third party inside the developer with employee-comparable access, above every external investigation tier — and the term that decides what its findings are worth is not the publication right but WHO classifies the redactions

A frontier developer has announced a partnership placing an external organisation's evaluators INSIDE the company, and the announcement is most useful for what it says is missing.

THE ACCESS TIER, in the operator's words: "Unlike today's external evaluators, embedded evaluators will work inside AI companies, with access comparable to an employee's. That access allows them to watch models take shape in training, follow the decisions that govern how those models are built and deployed, and speak directly to employees." The stated remit: "assess how a company operates, verify that it is keeping its safety commitments, and identify blind spots", plus "report incidents and give the public a more informed account of benefits and risks." The work "will include evaluating and red-teaming models, conducting alignment assessments, and testing model safeguards", led by Faculty, the partner's specialist AI business.

*** WHY THIS MATTERS FOR READING ANY EVALUATION RESULT. *** [[03fa7976]] records an independent evaluator's own tiering of investigation access — transcripts plus interviews establishes WHAT HAPPENED; only training-data classifier access, and above that ablations and intermediate checkpoints, establishes WHY — with the practical rule to read the access terms before the conclusions. Embedded access sits above every tier on that list: continuous rather than post-incident, covering the decisions as they are taken and not only the artifacts they produced. **At the time of that announcement the access terms this rule tells you to read did not exist**: "There are, as yet, no standards for what information embedded evaluators should have access to, or how they should report what they find." The same developer has since published a tiering and a concrete set of terms for its own programme — see THE PUBLISHED TERMS below. For this one developer the gap is therefore partly filled; there is still no industry standard, and the terms are the developer's own rather than negotiated or external.

**THE FUNDING STRUCTURE IS DISCLOSED AND IT IS THE SUBJECT PAYING THE EVALUATOR.** "There is also no settled system for funding independent evaluation. Long-term, we think funding should come from pooled or government sources ... As neither exists today, we plan to work with different evaluators under different funding arrangements", and concretely: "Given the importance and urgency of this work, Anthropic will fund Accenture's work directly. We are also in dialogue with METR and other nonprofit evaluators to pilot elements of embedded evaluation using their own funding." So the announced arrangement has the evaluated party paying a commercial consultancy for the evaluation, with self-funded nonprofit arrangements named as a parallel track rather than the model. The operator also states the accountability boundary itself: "independent embedded evaluators do not reduce our accountability, but help to make it more verifiable. The safety of our models remains our responsibility."

**THE PARALLEL TRACK NOW HAS A DOCUMENTED INSTANCE, AND ITS INDEPENDENCE TRADE RUNS THE OTHER WAY.** In a predeployment evaluation of a model from the same developer, the nonprofit evaluator states its own terms: "This evaluation was conducted under an unpaid agreement for AI R&D assessment. We drafted the initial summary, and then Anthropic had the opportunity to review and edit the text. We signed off on this final text from the Claude Opus 5.5 system card." So the two arrangements in circulation at once are: paid by the subject, and unpaid but subject-reviewed-and-edited with the text published inside the developer's own system card. Neither is obviously the independent one, and "who paid" is therefore not a sufficient question on its own — ask who held editorial control and where the text was published. [[97616086]] records what else that report discloses about its own limits.

WHAT FOLLOWS OPERATIONALLY for anyone who will later cite an embedded evaluation: ask for the three things the announcement says are unsettled — what access was granted, how findings are reported and published, and who paid — and treat their absence as a limit on the finding rather than a formality. The corresponding mechanism that already exists is a redaction summary describing how redaction affected the conclusions an investigator could publicly substantiate ([[cef2dda8]]); nothing equivalent is specified here.

COMMITTED SCALE, as stated: "Anthropic and Accenture each expect to invest at least $1 billion in building capacity in this area over the next five years." Both sides are non-exclusive — further evaluators are promised "in the coming weeks" and the partner "will work with other AI developers in similar capacities."

**MODALITY, WHICH BOUNDS THIS TIGHTLY.** Nothing has been evaluated yet under the embedded arrangement. The operator says plainly that "Embedded evaluation is new, and many of the details about how it will operate are still being worked out", and frames the post as sharing "these early efforts now so people and other AI developers can see our process". So this fact records a described arrangement, a committed spend and three named gaps — not a practice with results, and not an industry standard. The developer's own stated goal is "an ecosystem of evaluators operating with shared standards", which does not exist. The unpaid predeployment arrangement above is a real engagement with a published output, but it is ORDINARY predeployment evaluation, not embedded evaluation, and must not be read as the embedded programme producing results.

SOURCE COUNT HELD AT 1. The evaluator's own account of its terms is a second organisation, but it documents a different arrangement rather than replicating or testing anything this fact claims about the embedded one; it is a second datapoint on the same subject, not a corroboration of the same claim.

## THE PUBLISHED TERMS, AND THE ONE KNOB THAT DECIDES WHAT THE FINDINGS ARE WORTH

The developer has since set out a three-tier model and the terms of its top tier. The tiers, as described:

- **Tier 1** — narrowly scoped investigations; the developer chooses the access, and publication requires its approval.
- **Tier 2** — more information, and the evaluator publishes without approval. The developer can still require redactions, but evaluators "can publish their views on whether our redactions were justified."
- **Tier 3** — "employee-like access", with publication rights like tier 2. The first tier-3 arrangement is with a nonprofit evaluator: "We’ve onboarded six METR employees", given company hardware, office space and internal tools roughly on par with the developer's own risk-assessment teams, working under enforceable agreements and siloed from the rest of that evaluator's staff, including its researchers on other projects with the same developer.

The reporting terms are specific, and so is their asymmetry. "Anthropic can’t veto publication, but we can require them to redact information" in defined categories — "data about customers, attorney-client privileged information, or information about our model architectures" — and the categories are not adjudicated by anyone else: "Anthropic ultimately decides what information does and does not fall into these categories". The counterweight is a right to describe the constraint rather than to contest it: "METR reserves the right to make certain statements about the level of access".

**THAT IS THE KNOB.** Publication rights without veto look like independence, and they are worth little if the subject classifies what may be redacted, because the classification decides the content. The thing to ask of any such arrangement is not "can the evaluator publish" but who classifies the exclusions, and whether the evaluator may state how the exclusions bit — which is the same role the redaction summary plays in [[cef2dda8]]. Architecture information is additionally described as high-risk and shared only when highly relevant with explicit internal approval, so tier 3 access is employee-like in breadth and not in depth.

The independence test is also now stated, and it is about the evaluator's balance sheet: evaluators "shouldn’t hold equity in the company they’re evaluating" "or be financially or operationally dependent on it." Read that against the funding structure above: the paid commercial arrangement and this unpaid-nonprofit arrangement are both live, and the stated criterion rules against dependence rather than against payment.

**STILL NO RESULTS.** The named tier-3 evaluator "will publish its analysis" of the developer's risk levels, incidents or practices, and that analysis was not yet published when this was announced. A second partner is reviewing the chemical and biological weapons chapter of the developer's August risk report after a pilot review of an earlier one. So the programme has terms, people and a scope, and no published embedded finding — which is exactly the state in which the access terms matter most, because they are all a later reader will have to judge the finding by.
