# What an independent misalignment investigation needs, tiered by assurance: transcripts plus interviews answers what happened; only training-data access and ablations answer why

METR has published a template scope for a third-party investigation into the MOTIVES behind an agent misalignment incident — the evaluator's side of a question this pack otherwise holds only operator answers to. Its most usable content is the access list, because access is what separates tiers of assurance rather than merely making the work faster.

FOR A COMPREHENSIVE INVESTIGATION, four affordances, all four named:
1. The ability to RUN all models involved in the incident, to reproduce and investigate behaviour in similar situations.
2. Access to full transcripts, or to environments that let researchers closely reproduce the incident.
3. The ability to conduct EMPLOYEE INTERVIEWS, and METR names three groups: staff overseeing security and infrastructure (how the incident unfolded), staff overseeing training data and RL (how training produced it), and staff involved in any internal investigation.
4. The ability to run PROMPTED CLASSIFIERS OVER THE TRAINING DATA, or otherwise answer "how frequently did similar incidents occur during training?" and "were there environments that rewarded similar behavior as was seen in the incident?"
Plus an adequate inference budget, sufficient time, and access to effective AI tools.

THE TIERING IS THE POINT, AND IT IS WHAT A NEGOTIATED SCOPE WILL TRY TO BLUR. METR states that a faster investigation with transcripts and employee interviews alone "would provide more limited assurance and be insufficient to answer many important questions about the root causes and extent of the underlying misaligned behaviors". Above the comprehensive tier sits a further one: the ability to test ABLATIONS of the training data — remove some environments, run a limited training run, and see what changes — plus tests on intermediate training checkpoints and previous related models. So an engagement granted transcripts and interviews can establish WHAT HAPPENED and cannot establish WHY, and an investigation that reports root causes on that access is reporting beyond its evidence. The practical consequence for anyone commissioning or reading one: read the access terms before the conclusions, and treat a root-cause claim made without training-data access as an inference rather than a finding.

SHARING AND REDACTION, stated as requirements. Results go directly to the company's board and any other oversight bodies tasked with assessing safety practices, who "should have an opportunity to discuss the findings with the investigator and ask questions, including by sharing additional materials they received from the company with the investigator for comment." Conclusions should be public subject to company redactions for IP and confidential information, with "full transparency about the terms of the engagement, including at least redaction terms, access provided, time and personnel provided, and agreed upon scope" — and the investigator should provide a REDACTION SUMMARY "describing how the redaction process impacted the set of conclusions it could publicly substantiate". That last mechanism is the one [[cef2dda8]] records METR actually exercising in the Hugging Face report, so the proposal is not untested in that respect.

COST, stated: "A full investigation with the above scope may take weeks or months to complete", and METR suggests initial investigations may deliberately target a narrower scope for speed.

MODALITY, and it matters for how much weight this carries. This is a PROPOSAL and a template — METR's account of how such an investigation could work and what it would want — not a report of an investigation conducted under these terms, and not an agreed industry standard. METR states it "would be excited to work with AI companies" on significant incidents and is "building capacity to conduct these investigations more systematically".
