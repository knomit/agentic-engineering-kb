# OpenAI's misalignment reporting framework: employee-initiated, three tracks, deadlines per step, and a stated bias toward disclosing before the behaviour is explained

Published 2026-09-16 with six inaugural reports. The mechanics, which are the transferable part:

**Initiation is bottom-up.** "Any OpenAI employee may flag a misalignment example for investigation by our safety and alignment teams and request that it be considered for public disclosure." That starts the process, "with deadlines for each step to ensure timely investigation and disclosure."

**Triage into three tracks.** *Ready for Disclosure* (investigation sufficiently complete, publish after review), *Minor Investigation* (needs further technical work), and *Larger Investigation* — the "Slow Track" — for complex cases, "especially those involving third parties." OpenAI expects the first two to cover the large majority; all six inaugural reports fall in them. On the Slow Track, "our security, legal, and responsible disclosure obligations take precedence over this framework", an initial notice goes out as early as possible and may be delayed for security reasons, and that notice must give a high-level account, say whether outside experts are assisting, and estimate when a final report is expected.

**Escalation is named.** Disagreements about whether or how to disclose go to the Safety Advisory Group; disagreements within SAG, or staff objections to its decisions, escalate to leadership. The flagging employee is told the decision and the track.

**Report contents.** Every report states the behaviour, its severity and any external impact, the setting, the date or date range, when it was discovered, and the models involved at a high level. Where possible it adds further detail and resulting harm, how the misalignment was discovered and the scope of investigation, implications for alignment research, important unanswered questions, and planned measures — which "may not always be available at the time of disclosure, since we may publish the misalignment report before completing our investigation or developing a fix."

**Three design choices worth stealing.** (1) The framework "favors disclosure even when significance is uncertain", accepting that "some of the instances we disclose could prove to be spurious and not part of a larger pattern." (2) Recurrence of an already-disclosed issue is itself disclosable — "Repetition of the issue might itself be useful evidence about ... the effectiveness of our safeguards" — published by updating the original report rather than filing a new one. (3) The scheme is self-classifying against its own history: "The OpenAI Hugging Face incident would have fallen under this track had it been disclosed under this framework", i.e. Larger Investigation.

**The misreading to head off, which the source itself pre-empts:** "These are reports of individual instances, and shouldn't be considered reflective of how often misalignment occurs across our models." A count of published reports is a publication rate, not a misalignment rate, and a quarter with more reports may mean better flagging rather than worse models.

Context OpenAI states alongside it: "We do not believe that the AI industry has solved alignment and monitoring to a sufficient degree to continue responsibly scaling at maximum speed for much longer", that it considers the framework "a first step" toward industry-wide standards it intends to develop "with other developers, external researchers, industry standards bodies, and regulators", and that it believes serious safety, security and misalignment incidents "should be shared with the US federal government", with reporting mechanisms being proposed. The framework is stated to be complementary to, not a replacement for, existing legal disclosure requirements.

**NOT ESTABLISHED:** the deadlines are said to exist but their lengths are not published, and the framework has no track record — it launched with its first batch.
