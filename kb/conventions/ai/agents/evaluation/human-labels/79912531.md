# Raise the agreement of human labels before measuring a model against them: pair-discussion plus confidence filtering moved estimated agreement 53% to 68%, and a score-gap filter to 77%

On subjective outputs, human labels are not found, they are manufactured, and their agreement rate is the ceiling of every model score measured against them. TASTE's construction gives a replicable recipe with numbers for each step.

**The three moves, in order, and what each bought:**

1. **A pair-discussion stage.** Four researchers scored each item individually, then discussed their disagreements in pairs, then revised their scores. Combined with step 2 this raised estimated agreement by 15 percentage points, from 53% pre-discussion to 68%. Inspecting the discussion transcripts showed the disagreements split between substantive ones (how well a technique would work) and mundane ones (a rater misread the text the first time) — the second kind is pure noise that discussion removes for free.
2. **Filter on self-reported confidence.** Raters reported a per-item confidence; only "strong"-confidence labels were retained.
3. **Filter on the margin.** Keep only pairs whose overall scores differ by at least two points on a five-point scale. This carried the retained set to 77% estimated agreement, over 92 pairs, with each item capped at ten appearances so the data points stay independent.

The cost of steps 2 and 3 is coverage: the hard, close cases — often the ones that matter — are precisely what gets filtered out. A benchmark built this way measures judgment on items where experts can agree, and says nothing about the contested middle.

**A trap in reading the headline number.** Two defensible definitions of inter-rater agreement give different answers on the same data. Sampling one rater's score per item from the opposing discussion pair — approximating "a different person scores each item" — yields 77% across all 92 pairs. The more conventional measure, comparing only pairs where a second researcher scored both items, yields 83% but validates only 50 of the 92. The higher number is not a better benchmark; it is a narrower one. Report which estimator produced the figure and how many pairs it covers, because a reader comparing two benchmarks' agreement rates is otherwise comparing two different quantities.
