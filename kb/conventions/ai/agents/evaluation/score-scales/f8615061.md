# Score an unverifiable benchmark on a scale anchored at random guessing and at an inter-rater-agreement ceiling, not at 0 and 100

The Conceptual Reasoning Index scores models on tasks with no verifiable answers: rating arguments against a detailed rubric, remaining logically consistent between separate answers, and answering handcrafted decision-theory questions. Because its ground truth is expert ratings rather than a key, the scale is anchored at both ends. The bottom is chance, "with 0 corresponding to random guessing". The top is not perfect agreement but a ceiling "which we estimate based on expert inter-rater agreement", because the human ratings are themselves noisy.

The index also excludes what it cannot yet score cleanly rather than scoring it badly: "Currently, only models' performance at judging arguments goes into the CRI". Argument generation is evaluated — one model writes an argument and a second, given the rubric and few-shot examples, rates it — but that arm does not feed the index. The argument-rating benchmark is weighted most heavily, the consistency and decision-theory benchmarks lower, and the authors state that the weights and the benchmark set will be revised over time.

Operational consequence: for any eval graded against noisy human labels, publish both anchors alongside the score, and state the weighting if the score is a composite. Without the random-guessing floor a reader cannot distinguish a weak result from chance. Without the inter-rater ceiling, a reader reads the gap between the score and 100 as headroom — so a model already at the ceiling looks like it has room to improve, and a benchmark that is in fact saturated looks like it still discriminates.

What this does not mean: the ceiling is an estimate of what agreement with noisy raters allows, not a proof of maximum achievable capability, and it moves if the rating protocol is improved. Raising rater agreement raises the ceiling, so the two are design choices in the same system rather than independent facts.
