# A rubric-graded eval can be gamed by writing more, and the production remedy is an explicit per-500-character penalty calibrated SEPARATELY for each eval — the coefficient varied nearly 20x across four variants of the same benchmark family

Any benchmark that scores an open-ended response against a rubric of positive criteria rewards length mechanically: a longer answer has more opportunities to satisfy a criterion, so "long answers can also be used to artificially increase scores, without underlying improvements in usability and safety in real-world use." OpenAI's remedy in its GPT-6 Astra system card is an explicit, published length adjustment, and its shape is the part worth copying.

THE PROCEDURE. Responses of 2,000 characters receive no adjustment. Longer responses are penalised at a rate per 500 additional characters; shorter responses receive a corresponding POSITIVE adjustment, so the correction is two-sided rather than a cap. Models are not told about the length penalty in their prompts, which is what stops the adjustment from becoming a new target to optimise against. Reported scores are the adjusted ones, with the unadjusted score and the mean response length in characters printed alongside, so a reader can see how much of a delta the adjustment accounts for.

THE PENALTY COEFFICIENT IS PER-EVAL, NOT GLOBAL, AND THE SPREAD IS LARGE. On the same 0-100 scale, per 500 additional characters: 1.47 points for HealthBench Professional, 2.99 for HealthBench, 3.92 for HealthBench Hard, 0.20 for HealthBench Consensus. That is a range of nearly 20x across four variants of ONE benchmark family, which means length sensitivity is a property of the individual rubric and its criteria, not of the domain or the task format. A single normalisation constant applied across a suite would under-correct the sensitive evals and over-correct the insensitive ones. Calibrate per eval, and publish the coefficient so the adjustment is auditable.

THE TENSION TO KEEP IN VIEW, which the source states itself: "Longer answers may be better when they include additional valuable information, but they also have more opportunities to satisfy positive rubric criteria, and unnecessarily long responses can be less useful to end users and clinicians." A length penalty therefore charges for genuine added content as well as for padding, and it cannot distinguish them. It is a bias correction on the measurement, not a judgement about the response.

A RELATED RETIREMENT CRITERION FROM THE SAME CARD. The operator recommends moving off the older variant of its own benchmark because that variant "is approaching a noise ceiling for frontier models", and it justifies the successor on PREDICTIVENESS rather than on difficulty: improvements on it "have been much more predictive of improvements in other held-out evaluations in our internal testing". So the test for whether a benchmark still earns its place is whether movement on it predicts movement on held-out evals, not whether the absolute scores still have headroom.

WHAT THIS DOES NOT MEAN: the adjustment is not a substitute for a rubric that penalises padding directly, and it does not make scores from an unadjusted run comparable to adjusted ones. A cited score from a length-adjusted eval must say whether it is the adjusted or the unadjusted figure, because in this card the two differ by up to 5 points in either direction.
