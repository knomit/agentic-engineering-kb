# Model-generated eval items are candidates, not items: close to 14,000 generated consistency constraints yielded 567 human-approved ones, and only those 567 score

ACCoRD, one of three benchmarks aggregated into Anthropic's Conceptual Reasoning Index, checks whether a model's stated probabilities and preference orderings are logically consistent across separate queries. Its items were generated by a model and then gated by hand: "The ACCoRD dataset contains close to 14,000 model-generated consistency constraints"; "Of these, 567 were further checked and approved by us."; "We include only those 567 constraints in our aggregate conceptual reasoning performance metric, the CRI." The generated pool passed through an automated checker first; human approval was a further stage on top of it. The hand-built companion benchmark in the same index is a different shape and a different scale: DTBench "is a dataset of 407 handcrafted multiple-choice questions", expert-validated, with a further 130 attitude questions excluded from the index.

Operational consequence: when you generate eval items with a model, human review is the rate-limiting step and the thing to budget for, and the plan should assume a yield in the low single-digit percent of the generated pool rather than a filter that keeps most of it. Score on the approved subset only; keep the unapproved pool for exploration, error analysis and regeneration, and never let it reach the headline metric. The generated pool's size is not the eval's size, and reporting it as though it were overstates the eval by more than an order of magnitude here.

What this does not mean: the discarded constraints are not established as wrong. The source says the 567 were "further checked and approved", not that the rest were refuted. The reason to exclude them from the metric is noise, not demonstrated error — so a generated pool is still worth keeping and is still worth generating at scale.
