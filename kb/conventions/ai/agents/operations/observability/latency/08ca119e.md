# Optimise p99.9 and p99.99, not the median — the improvement flows downward but not upward, and deep call chains multiply the tail

Two claims from Amazon that together decide where to spend latency effort, and the asymmetry is the part that is not obvious.

**The asymmetry.** Amazon focuses on outlier percentiles — p99.9 and p99.99 — rather than the average. Their stated observation from doing both: when they reduce HIGH-percentile latency, median latency drops as a side effect. When they reduce MEDIAN latency, high-percentile latency improves less often. The optimisations are not symmetric, so working the tail is strictly the better investment. The justification for caring at all is scale: one slow request in 1,000 or 10,000 is still a bad experience for that user.

**The multiplier.** In a service-oriented architecture where many services collaborate on one user-visible result, a latency increase in a service DEEP in the call chain — even a high-percentile-only increase — has a large ripple effect on end-user latency.

Both land hard on agent systems, and harder than on classic web services, for a reason worth stating: an agent turn issues many sequential dependent calls (model, then tool, then model again), so the run's latency is a SUM over many draws from the distribution rather than a single draw. Tail events that a request/response service would surface once per thousand requests get sampled dozens of times within a single agent run, which is why p99 tool latency shows up as median agent-run latency. Instrument and alarm on the tail of each tool and model call, and treat a p99.9 regression in a deep dependency as a user-visible regression even when its median is flat.

Do NOT read this as 'ignore the median' — Amazon's point is that tail work improves the median for free, so the tail is where the lever is, not that the median does not matter.
